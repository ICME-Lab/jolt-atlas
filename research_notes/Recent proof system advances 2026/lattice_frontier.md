## 1. Akita: commitment relation, extraction theorem, polynomial classes, digit range check, costs

### Takeaway
Akita's knowledge-soundness theorem gives **no norm bound of any kind on the committed (root) polynomial**: the extractor returns an arbitrary F_q evaluation vector plus an "algebraic decommitment" whose source digits are explicitly *not* required to be short or canonical. The internal digit range check certifies only the *next recursive witness* (responses, partials, inner-image digits), never the application's coefficients; "bounded dense" is a prover-side sizing contract that makes commitment cheaper, not a verified statement.

### Cited Findings
- Three witness shapes exist: (1) full-field, arbitrary F_q coefficients, gadget depth `ceil(log_b q)`; (2) small balanced digits in `{-b/2..b/2-1}`, depth 1, "the shape of every recursive witness"; (3) one-hot, blocks with exactly one coefficient equal to 1, depth 1. — [Akita paper §4.1](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Remark 4.1 (verbatim substance): the small-digit shape *of recursive witnesses* is validated inside the protocol by the digit range check; the one-hot shape is validated *outside* the protocol by the surrounding proof system (Jolt's booleanity and Hamming-weight sum-checks). "The external one-hot guarantee is used to sharpen the honest-response bound, not to prove Akita knowledge soundness. The Akita extractor recovers a polynomial together with the algebraic decommitment of Definition 10.11, whether or not that polynomial is one-hot." — [Akita paper §4.1, Remark 4.1](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Definition 10.11 (commitment relation `Rel_com^Akita`): source blocks satisfy `f* = Dec_shape((s_i))` and `A s_i = G_{b1} t̂_i`; the inner-image digits `t̂` and compression-chain digits lie in their alphabets; the relation "requires neither the canonical gadget decomposition for `s` nor membership of the decoded vector in the image of an honest padding map." No bound on `s_i` or on `f*` appears in the relation. — [Akita paper §10.2, Def. 10.11](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Definition 10.12 (weak opening): the extracted source `s` is bounded only through a product with a short unit: `||ι(c̄)||_1 ≤ κ̄_1` and `||ι(c̄)·s||_∞,coef ≤ β̄_∞` (plus l2 variants on a Euclidean route). — [Akita paper Def. 10.12](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Remark 10.30 ("What knowledge extraction does not claim"): "The source vector recovered by fold-challenge subtraction contains an inverse challenge difference and need not be the canonical, short gadget decomposition used by the honest commitment algorithm. … A stronger claim that the extractor recovers the canonical commitment state would require an additional canonicality or source-shortness argument. It does not follow from the bounded folded responses, because division by a unit need not preserve norm … Akita does not use that stronger claim." — [Akita paper Remark 10.30](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- §2.1 states the same at overview level: after describing the range check, "This check concerns the successor witness; the source recovered by extraction requires a separate argument", and "an inverse of a short ring element need not be short. Extraction instead bounds the product `Δc_i · s_i`; this is a weak opening." — [Akita paper §2.1](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Theorems 10.29/10.31: the extractor returns either a witness in `Rel_batch^Akita` (decoded full-grid vectors with correct evaluations at the opening points) or a short Module-SIS kernel vector for one matrix; FS knowledge soundness holds in the classical ROM with error `(Q+1)·ε_int + Δ_setup + Σ Adv_MSIS`. No QROM proof; no zero knowledge. — [Akita paper §10.2, §14](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Norm type used internally: the digit range check is an exact per-entry l-infinity statement (every Boolean evaluation of the next-level witness lies in `{-b*/2..b*/2-1}`, `b* ∈ {4,8,16,32,64}`); optionally a sum-check certifies an **exact** (not approximate) total squared l2 norm of the response. "Akita uses no Johnson–Lindenstrauss projection." — [Akita paper §1.1, §1.2, §6.1](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Remark 10.14: collision norms are priced at the alphabet the protocol *enforces* (`b*−1`), not the one the honest prover uses; a level's range check certifies the common alphabet and "does not identify the positional base of any segment". — [Akita paper Remark 10.14, §6.1](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Optimized digit range check: vanishing polynomial `Q_b(w)=Π_{a∈A_b}(w−a)` has degree b; pairing `(w−k)(w+k+1) = w(w+1) − k(k+1)` halves it to degree b/2 in `s = w(w+1)`; `s̃` is never committed, its claim is tied back to `w̃` inside the relation sum-check; for b* > 8 a GKR-style product tree with fan-in 2 or 4 (shapes: b=16 → degrees (2,4); 32 → (4,4); 64 → (2,4,4)). Prover cost drops from O(b²L) to O(bL); communication `2(log2 b − 1)·ceil(log2 L) + O(1)` challenge-field elements. — [Akita paper §1.1, §2.3, §6.1](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Root digit base: "For prover efficiency, we choose 3-bit balanced digits" `{-4..3}`. Code confirms `log_basis: 3` for all proof-optimized presets. — [Akita paper footnote 10](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf); `~/.cargo/git/checkouts/akita-967e7e5e12514df2/dfdbd76/crates/akita-config/src/proof_optimized.rs:160-162`
- Commitment structure: two-tier Ajtai. Pack `d_A` base-field coefficients per ring element, split into B blocks of M ring elements, `s_i = G^{-1}_{b,M}(f_i)` with depth `δ_com = max(1, ceil(log_b β_shape))`, inner `t_i = A s_i`, decompose `t̂_i`, sliced outer `u = B t̂`, then optional two-stage binary compression to a 128-byte public commitment (256-byte intermediate). — [Akita paper §4.5, Table 6](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Cost per class: full-field decomposes every coefficient to `ceil(log_b q)` digits (43 digits at b=8, q≈2^128 — arithmetic from the formula); small-digit and one-hot have depth 1. The one-hot backend "uses the source's sparse structure to visit only its nonzero monomial positions"; the dense backend does a "matrix product with CRT and NTT arithmetic". — [Akita paper §4.5](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf); `…/dfdbd76/book/src/how/commitment.md:294-300`
- K=16 vs K=256 families (Jolt layout): packed trace capacity C=64 slots for K=16 and C=32 for K=256; physical domains 2^(t+10) and 2^(t+13); "the prover constructs the commitment from sparse trace data without materializing the zero entries". Jolt switches from 4-bit to 8-bit committed one-hot chunks at T=2^25; at that switch Akita's proof shrinks 96.1→94.2 KB, verification rises 16.88→28.17 ms, prover throughput increases. — [Akita paper App. E.1, §13.4](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Batching: one heterogeneous proof opens several commitments formed at different times at their own points; "No commitment homomorphism or common point across objects is required." The certified digit alphabet is fixed before commitments are formed; all vectors inside one commitment share one coefficient envelope, and "vectors requiring different source digit depths must use separate commitment handles". — [Akita paper §1.1, §4.1, App. E.3](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Standalone benchmarks (AMD Ryzen 9 9950X, AVX-512, single thread, 32-bit field; "bits committed" = N·log2|F|), Table 9, Akita direct: 2^27 bits: commit 0.100 s, open 0.998 s, verify 7.6 ms, proof 61.3 KB; 2^31: 1.29 s / 2.64 s / 12.7 ms / 63.1 KB; 2^35: 24.6 s / 16.1 s / 33.3 ms / 64.6 KB, 4.69 GiB peak (Greyhound at 2^35: 59.4 s / 21.3 s / 1525 ms / 66.1 KB / 104.8 GiB; RoKoKo: 17.9 s / 4.38 s / 7.4 ms / 115.1 KB / 35.6 GiB). Setup offloading at 2^35: verify 33.3→16.2 ms, open 16.1→18.6 s, proof 64.6→67.3 KB. — [Akita paper Table 9](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Jolt end-to-end (Apple M4 Max, 16 cores, SHA-256 chains), Table 11: T=2^21: Akita 3.30 s prove / 11.66 ms verify / 92.8 KB vs Dory 5.63 s / 77.80 ms / 90.6 KB; T=2^27: Akita 90.85 s / 39.82 ms / 98.1 KB / 35.47 GiB vs Dory 175.18 s / 88.29 ms / 95.9 KB / 34.76 GiB. Throughput 1.477 MHz (Akita) vs 0.766 MHz (Dory) at 2^27; Akita 1.3–2.2x faster proving, 2.2–7.4x faster verification. — [Akita paper §13.4](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Authors' stated weaknesses: proving time "not yet on par with Greyhound and RoKoKo"; the response model for later-fold schedules is heuristic (completeness); no QROM analysis; no zero knowledge. — [Akita paper §14](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)

### Inferences
- The decoded root vector is `f = G_b·s` where `s` is only known to satisfy "(short unit)·s is short". Since `s = c̄^{-1}·(short)` can have arbitrary coefficients mod q, the theorem permits `f` to be any F_q vector. So the PCS gives neither an exact range `[0, 2^b)` nor a relaxed/slack bound nor an l2 bound on committed coefficients. The research-directions question "exact or slack-relaxed?" resolves to "neither is claimed".
- The internal range check cannot be repurposed as stated: it runs over the level-1 witness `(ẑ, ê, t̂, r̂)`, which contains the *folded response* digits (a random short-ring-combination of all source blocks), not the source digits `s_i` themselves. There is no point in the protocol where the root's own digits are individually range-checked.
- Under the dense-bounded flavor a 65-bit-bounded coefficient costs about `ceil(65/3) ≈ 22` base-8 digit ring coefficients in the A input (the code notes one extra digit may be needed); a hypothetical b-bit preset would cost about `ceil((b+1)/3)`. That is the sense in which Akita is "pay-per-bit" for dense data: cost scales with the *declared* width, by honest-prover contract.

### Gaps
- No microbenchmark exists (in the paper or found in the repo's `bench-data`, which holds field microbenches) of dense-bounded b-bit commit time versus one-hot digits for the same values on Fp128. Table 9/10 use uniformly dense inputs measured in "bits committed"; they do not isolate small-width dense columns.
- §3, §7–9, §11–12 and Appendices A–D, F, G of the paper were not read; nothing in the sections read suggests a source-shortness argument elsewhere, and Remark 10.30 says Akita does not use one.
- LayerZero blog posts on Akita were not searched; all Akita facts are from the paper and the pinned code.

## 2. Given the theorem: can jolt-atlas rely on the PCS for (a) an exact range or (b) a no-overflow bound, and what must be added

### Takeaway
No to both: neither exact `[0, 2^b)` nor a relaxed no-overflow bound follows from committing a column as dense-bounded. Any range property has to be proved by the surrounding PIOP on the committed polynomial, exactly as one-hot validity is today; the useful thing Akita contributes is a cheap template for that proof (a degree-b/2 zero-check on dense digit columns) and a cheaper commit for narrow dense columns.

### Cited Findings
- Akita's own framing of the division of labour: "Booleanity, one-hot validity, and the relations connecting decoded values to the execution remain obligations of the Jolt PIOP." — [Akita paper App. E.2](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Upstream Jolt already commits a small-valued column as dense-bounded instead of one-hot digits: PR #2145 (merged 2026-10-28) "commit RV64 increments directly to remove digit proof work": previously "balanced digit/carry one-hot columns. With K=16 this adds 17 columns to the native trace commitment, Booleanity, digit-zero reduction, and final opening. Commit the existing fused increment polynomial directly with the bounded-dense PCS and open its already-reduced ReadRAF claim." — [a16z/jolt#2145](https://github.com/a16z/jolt/pull/2145); cleanup in [a16z/jolt#2167](https://github.com/a16z/jolt/pull/2167)
- Two days later upstream merged PR #2190 "fix: range-check direct RAM loads", which routes the loaded value "into the interleaved right lookup operand, where instruction read-RAF proves it is a `u64`". — [a16z/jolt#2190](https://github.com/a16z/jolt/pull/2190)
- The range-check sum-check Akita uses on dense digit vectors: `Σ_x eq(τ,x)·Q_sq(w(x)(w(x)+1)) = 0`, degree b/2 in the derived value, valid because the check is on Boolean-hypercube evaluations of a committed multilinear polynomial. — [Akita paper §6.1 eq. (110)–(111)](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)

### Inferences
- What a construction would have to enforce to get an **exact** range from dense columns: commit the digits themselves as dense small-digit columns (e.g. three 4-bit digits of a 12-bit remainder as three dense cells, or six 2-bit/four 3-bit cells), and run an application-level zero-check `Q_b(d(x)) = 0` over the hypercube for each digit column, plus the existing recomposition identity. Soundness then rests on PCS evaluation binding plus the sum-check, not on any shortness of the extracted opening. This replaces, per 4-bit digit: one one-hot polynomial of 16 cells with Booleanity + Hamming-weight checks, by one dense cell with a degree-8 (in `s`) or product-tree zero-check. Committed field elements per digit drop 16x in domain size; whether commit *time* drops is a benchmark question because one-hot commit visits 1 nonzero per digit while a dense 4-bit cell costs about 2 base-8 gadget digits through the NTT/CRT dense path.
- For the upstream precedent (#2145) the reading is that the increments needed no range proof from the commitment: their values are pinned by other relations, and where a width bound was needed (#2190) it was obtained from a lookup, not from the bounded-dense flavor. This is consistent with the theorem; it is my reading of two PR descriptions, not a statement by the authors.
- A **relaxed no-overflow** bound could come from an explicit approximate norm argument added at the PIOP layer (random ±1/ternary combination of the column shown small, see section 4), but that also is not something Akita supplies; Akita deliberately uses no JL projection.
- Could Akita be modified to give it natively? One would need the root source digits `s_i` to be part of a range-checked witness. That means either (i) a "level-0" range check over all `N·δ_com` source digits, which is the application-level zero-check above moved inside the PCS and paid on every committed coefficient, or (ii) a source-shortness extraction argument, which Remark 10.30 says does not follow from the fold. Option (i) has no advantage over doing it in the PIOP, where it can be restricted to columns that need it.
- Practical prerequisites in the current code: a new commitment-config preset with a small `log_commit_bound` and its own offline-generated schedule catalog (presets are compile-time types with catalog identities; only 65 and 128 exist for dense fp128), and separate commitment handles per digit depth.

### Gaps
- Unknown without measurement: dense small-digit commit time per cell versus one-hot K=16 per digit on Fp128 at GPT-2 trace volume, and the cost of the degree-8 zero-check versus the current one-hot Booleanity/Hamming-weight path. This is step 1 of `docs/research-directions.md` §9 and remains the deciding experiment.
- Whether the Akita planner can produce good schedules for very narrow dense bounds (e.g. 4–5 signed bits) was not checked.

## 3. Pay-per-bit lattice commitments: Neo and SuperNeo

### Takeaway
Only the abstracts could be retrieved. They establish that pay-per-bit comes from an Ajtai commitment applied to a low-norm (digit/bit) matrix form of the witness, over small fields such as Goldilocks, with norm checks run by sum-check; they do not let me state the extracted-witness guarantee or any benchmark, so whether SuperNeo's guarantee is exact per-coefficient is still unverified.

### Cited Findings
- Neo (received 2025-02-20): lattice folding scheme for CCS adapting HyperNova; "pay-per-bit" Ajtai commitment whose cost scales with the bit-width of the committed scalars: committing to a vector of bits is 32x cheaper than to a vector of 32-bit values; achieved by transforming the vector into a matrix and committing to the matrix, shown to retain the linear homomorphism needed for folding; works over small prime fields such as Goldilocks; a single sum-check over an extension of the small field. Subsumed by 2026/242. — [ePrint 2025/294 abstract page](https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/294)
- Neo and SuperNeo (Nguyen, Setty; received 2026-02-13, revised 2026-09-04; CRYPTO 2026): targets six properties: plausible post-quantum security, pay-per-bit commitment costs, field-native arithmetic, non-SIMD constraint systems, small-field support (e.g. Goldilocks), low recursion overhead. Neo meets five (needs SIMD constraint systems); SuperNeo meets all six. Mechanism per abstract: "new folding-friendly instantiations of Ajtai commitments under the Module-SIS assumption" and "two new norm-preserving embeddings of field vectors into ring vectors that respect an evaluation homomorphism required for folding"; sum-check and norm checks run over a small field extension; SuperNeo natively supports CCS over arbitrary extension fields of the commitment's field without an NTT embedding. — [ePrint 2026/242 abstract page](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/242)
- No benchmarks, parameters or norm bounds are stated on either abstract page. — [ePrint 2026/242](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/242); [ePrint 2025/294](https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/294)
- Akita's authors place Neo/SuperNeo in the folding line ("obtained pay-per-bit commitment costs over small fields") and use "SuperNeo's framework" to explain part of their extraction proof structure. — [Akita paper §1.2, §10.2](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Pay-per-bit" in Neo/SuperNeo and in Akita is the same phenomenon: Ajtai is a linear map applied to a small-integer decomposition, so cost follows the number and size of digits. In all cases read so far it is a *cost* property; a range guarantee comes only from a separate norm/range check inside the folding protocol.
- In a folding scheme the range check is on the witness being folded (the application witness), which is different from Akita, where the checked object is the recursive PCS witness. So a folding scheme of this family is the more plausible place to find "range comes with the commitment" for application values. This is an inference from the abstracts' mention of norm checks, not a read fact.
- Composition with jolt-atlas: the abstracts describe small fields (Goldilocks-size) with extension-field sum-check; nothing read says a 128-bit prime is unsupported, and Akita itself shows Module-SIS Ajtai over a 128-bit prime is practical. But Neo/SuperNeo are folding schemes for CCS, not PCSs for a sum-check IOP; adopting them means recasting per-layer checks as CCS instances to fold, which is an architectural change, not a drop-in.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved: the SuperNeo PDF (403 from eprint by curl and WebFetch; archive unreachable). Unknown from primary text: the exact norm statement of the extracted witness (exact l-infinity vs relaxed), decomposition base and depth, ring/modulus choices, any 128-bit-field instantiation, concrete prover costs, and implementation status.

## 4. Algebraic range proofs in lattice folding; exact versus approximate norm proofs

### Takeaway
The 2025–2026 line splits into exact l-infinity checks by sum-check (Hachi, Akita, Cyclo, LatticeFold+'s algebraic monomial proof) and approximate l2 checks by random projection (LaBRADOR, Greyhound, Grand Danois, PikkuFold, 2026/721), with a trend toward checking fresh inputs only and toward l2-plus-exact-shortening. Slack factors could be confirmed only for Greyhound's JL check (2, corrected by the Akita authors to sqrt(128/29) ≈ 2.1); the rest were read at abstract level.

### Cited Findings
- LatticeFold required a bit-decomposition range proof on all input witnesses; LatticeFold+ (Boneh, Chen; CRYPTO 2025) replaces it with "a new purely algebraic range proof", uses double commitments to shrink proofs, and claims a prover "five to ten times faster" than LatticeFold, with a simpler verifier circuit and shorter proofs; LatticeFold works with 64-bit fields. — [ePrint 2025/247 abstract page](https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/247)
- Cyclo (Garreta, Lipmaa, Luhaäär, Osadnik; EUROCRYPT 2026, revised 2026-04-13): avoids norm checks on the accumulator; range checks only on the input (non-accumulated) witness via an l-infinity range test built on sum-check; witness norm grows additively per round for a bounded number of folds; proof sizes on the order of 30 KB, an order of magnitude below LatticeFold+. — [ePrint 2026/359 abstract page](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/359)
- Osadnik, "Improving LatticeFold+ with l2-norm checks" (received 2026-04-13): replaces "expensive l-infinity range checks" with random-projection constraints "in the spirit of RoK and Roll" plus an exact shortening step "in the spirit of SALSAA" that recovers the original l2 bound at extraction; claims substantially lower prover cost on the norm-check path; no numbers on the abstract page. — [ePrint 2026/721 abstract page](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/721)
- PikkuFold (Osadnik, 2026/1809): layered random projections, no in-protocol commitment beyond fresh inputs, about 5.5 KB per folding step versus at least 30 KB for Cyclo and at least 60 KB for SALSAA (search snippet of the abstract); Akita's authors add that it "proves a concrete modular Johnson–Lindenstrauss theorem". — [ePrint 2026/1809](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1809); [Akita paper §1.2](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Grand Danois integrates a Johnson–Lindenstrauss norm proof into the Greyhound relation, under Module-SIS plus vanishing SIS, estimating about 80–90 KB proofs at 2^32 with no implementation. — [Akita paper §1.2, Table 1](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Greyhound's JL norm check uses slack 2; the Akita authors state "the original dense-sign projection does not match the distribution required by the certified lower-tail bound" and substitute a sparse-ternary projection with slack sqrt(128/29). — [Akita paper §13.2](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Hachi/Akita: l-infinity digit membership by sum-check on a degree-b (Hachi) or degree-b/2 (Akita) vanishing polynomial; Akita's optional l2 check is exact, "we verify exact, not approximate L2 norm". — [Akita paper §1.1](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Akita's Appendix F is cited by the authors as recording corrections to prior protocol descriptions and implementations, and they cite a security review that found critical vulnerabilities in several open-source lattice argument implementations. — [Akita paper §1 footnote 5, §1.2](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)

### Inferences
- FlashProve's "random sums over a whole matrix" (as described in `docs/research-directions.md` §7.3; not re-read) and the LaBRADOR-family JL projection are the same principle: a random small-coefficient linear combination of a vector is small if the vector is small, and is large or wraps with high probability otherwise. Differences that matter for jolt-atlas: (i) lattice JL proofs target an l2 bound with a constant slack and need many projections (LaBRADOR-style uses 256) sent or committed; (ii) over a prime field the test is modular, so it only works when the claimed bound is far below q (a "modular JL" lemma), which is comfortable at 128 bits for 32–40-bit values; (iii) both give approximate bounds, hence suit no-overflow, never exact rounding remainders.
- For jolt-atlas this supports the split already proposed in research-directions §7.3: exact range (remainder) by an exact check; no-overflow (clamp slack) by a projection check. The lattice literature supplies the soundness template (modular JL with stated slack) for the second half; PikkuFold's concrete modular JL theorem and RoK-and-Roll's structured projections (verifier-succinct random vectors) are the two papers to read for the "random vector with succinctly evaluable MLE" design question.
- LatticeFold+'s monomial-encoding range proof is, structurally, a one-hot encoding in the exponent of a ring element (commit `X^a`, check constant-term relation). It is the ring analogue of one-hot lookups, cheap in a ring commitment because a monomial has one nonzero coefficient. That is the same cost profile jolt-atlas already has with one-hot digits, so it is not obviously a saving for a sum-check IOP over Fp128. This is inference from secondary descriptions; the paper was not read.

### Gaps
- Not read in full: LatticeFold, LatticeFold+, Hachi, Greyhound, LaBRADOR, Grand Danois, PikkuFold, Cyclo, Symphony, ProtogaLattice, RoK and Roll, SALSAA. Exact slack factors and projection counts for LaBRADOR/RoK-and-Roll/PikkuFold are therefore not cited here.
- Whether any 2026 scheme gives an exact per-coefficient range on fresh inputs at lower cost than a degree-b/2 sum-check zero-check was not established.

## 6. Lattice-based or lattice-accelerated zkML, and GPU lattice commitments (2026)

### Takeaway
I found no 2026 lattice-based zkML system other than jolt-atlas itself, and no GPU implementation of a lattice polynomial commitment or folding scheme with reported throughput. The nearest relevant 2026 results are CPU-only: LaBinius (lattice commitment for binary-field witnesses within 1.2x of hash-based provers) and Akita's planned Metal backend.

### Cited Findings
- A web search for lattice-based zkML in 2026 returned no matching system; results were Halo2/IPA and GKR-based zkML work (e.g. Artemis, arXiv 2409.12055). — [search result: Artemis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12055)
- LaBinius (Osadnik, Seiler; received 2026-09-18): lattice PCS that "separates the evaluation arithmetic from the commitment arithmetic": commitment over a composite modulus with NTT-friendly prime factors, evaluation claim in a binary extension field with no bitness checks; plugs into Binius and Flock front ends almost black-box; AVX-512; single core: prover at most 1.2x the hash-based prover on Keccak-256, SHA-256, BLAKE3; over 67,000 BLAKE3 compressions per second; verification under 40 ms; with LaBRADOR as recursive compressor, under 2x the hash-based provers and proofs under 100 KiB, about 5x smaller than Binius/Flock. — [ePrint 2026/2103 abstract page](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2103)
- Maltese (Cheng, Nguyen, Tyagi; ASIACRYPT 2026): succinct PCS over a Merkle tree of Ajtai hashes, standard Module-SIS, 335 KB openings at N = 2^30. — [ePrint 2026/2067 abstract page](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2067)
- Akita GPU status: Metal/GPU backend is open work (see section 5). Greyhound's reference implementation is "End-to-end C, AVX-512 only". — [Akita paper Table 1](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Akita's authors name applications they consider next: aggregating ML-DSA/Falcon signatures and verifiable FHE; zkML is not mentioned. — [Akita paper §14](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)

### Inferences
- LaBinius is the first concrete evidence against the "no known construction" entry for option C in research-directions §5: it links a lattice commitment in one characteristic to evaluation claims in a binary field. It does not give what option C needs (bits committed in a binary scheme, arithmetic in a 128-bit prime field), but it shows the commitment modulus and the evaluation field can be decoupled, and it is the reference to read before writing the option-C feasibility note.
- LaBinius also weakens the reading that lattice commitments are inherently slower per bit than hash-based ones: 1.2x on one core, with 5x smaller proofs. That is an abstract-level claim on hash workloads, not on small-integer tensors.
- Absence of GPU lattice PCS numbers means a lattice-stack GPU prover for zkML would be first of its kind; the dense path (NTT/CRT matrix products) is the GPU-friendly one, and the one-hot path (sparse gathers) is less so. This favours dense small-digit columns over one-hot digits if a GPU prover is ever a goal.

### Gaps
- No reliable source found for any GPU Ajtai/NTT commitment throughput in a proof-system context (2026). Generic GPU NTT papers exist but were not examined.
- The searches were two queries; a lattice zkML preprint could exist and have been missed. I found none.

## 7. Where lattices are structurally better than hash-based schemes, with current numbers

### Takeaway
On Akita's own measurements the lattice advantage is proof size (63–69 KB versus 114–933 KB at 2^31 bits) and sparse/small-value commitment cost; it is **not** verifier time (most hash-based verifiers are faster: 0.8–8 ms versus 10–13 ms) and not opening time. At the full-system level Jolt-Akita proofs stay under 100 KB with 10–40 ms verification, against FlashProve's 18.78 MB and 17 s.

### Cited Findings
- Hash-based comparison at 2^31 bits committed, eight prover threads (Table 10): Akita p32/p64/p128: commit 0.185/0.126/0.346 s, open 0.538/0.422/0.376 s, verify 12.7/10.9/10.5 ms, proof 63.1/66.7/68.3 KB, RAM 0.50–0.83 GiB. WHIR (Plonky3): 0.476 s / 6.25 s / 2.1 ms / 113.6 KB / 2.80 GiB. Plonky3 STIR: 0.674 / 1.27 / 3.3 ms / 164.9 KB. Binius64 BaseFold: 0.215 / 0.103 / 0.8 ms / 489.2 KB. Flock Ligerito: 0.124 / 0.300 / 1.1 ms / 494.6 KB. SP1 BaseFold: 2.49 / 2.30 / 20.3 ms / 933.0 KB. — [Akita paper Table 10](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- At 2^35 bits: Akita p128 2.89 s commit / 1.82 s open / 23.8 ms / 69.1 KB / 5.05 GiB; Flock Ligerito 2.38 / 5.21 / 1.7 ms / 570.5 KB / 21.7 GiB; Binius64 BaseFold 4.97 / 1.42 / 1.2 ms / 690.3 KB / 22.7 GiB; WHIR (Plonky3, unique decoding) 5.53 / 8.38 / 11.4 ms / 690.9 KB. — [Akita paper Table 10](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- The authors' own summary: "a proof-size/runtime tradeoff rather than one implementation winning every metric … Every hash baseline except SP1 also verifies faster than Akita". — [Akita paper §13.3](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Proven-soundness hash-based proofs are "at least 160KB for non-trivial instances" (WHIR, rate 1/2 or 1/4, no conjectured proximity gaps) and scale as `O(λ² log N + λ log² N / log log N)`; lattice openings are logarithmic and "as small as 50–60 KB". — [Akita paper §1](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Sparse commitment: "The dominant Reed–Solomon-and-Merkle construction instead commits to a dense error-corrected codeword, so its standard commitment procedure processes the entire codeword even when the original vector is sparse"; systematic codes (Brakedown, Bolt) "could benefit from one-hot or other highly repetitive structure, though the practical speedups remain unclear". — [Akita paper §1 and footnote 2](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Peak memory: Akita uses several times less RAM than the hash-based baselines at the largest sizes (about 5 GiB versus 12–73 GiB at 2^35 bits). — [Akita paper Table 10](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Homomorphism: Akita's batching does not use commitment homomorphism; the lattice folding line (LatticeFold, Neo/SuperNeo, Cyclo, Symphony, ProtogaLattice) is where linear homomorphism is exploited for IVC/PCD, and Akita's authors call extending Akita toward a practical folding scheme "exciting future work". — [Akita paper §1.2, App. E.3](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Preprocessing cost of the lattice route: beyond seeds and offloading commitments, an Akita verifier may retain an expanded public-matrix prefix of size `Õ(N^(1/κ_root))`. — [Akita paper §1.2](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Full-system contrast from the repo's own notes: FlashProve 18.78 MB per request, 17 s verification on 24 threads; jolt-atlas 2.4 MiB and about 2 s on GPT-2. — `/Users/kg/swe/icme/official/jolt-atlas/docs/research-directions.md` §7.1, §7.6 (figures not re-verified this round)

### Inferences
- `docs/research-directions.md` §7.6 says small proofs and fast verification are where a lattice commitment is structurally better. The PCS-level data supports "small proofs" and "low memory" but not "fast verification": a hash-based PCS verifies in 1–3 ms. FlashProve's 17 s verifier and 18.78 MB proof are consequences of its committed-trace architecture and scale, not of hash-based commitments as such. The defensible lattice claims to compete on are: proof size under 100 KB for the PCS part, aggregation-friendliness, and sparse/small-value commitment.
- jolt-atlas's 2.4 MiB proof is about 25x larger than a complete Jolt-Akita proof (86–98 KB). Since the Akita opening is about 65–85 KB, most of jolt-atlas's proof is presumably PIOP messages and per-polynomial material; proof size is therefore mostly a protocol-layout matter (number of sum-check stages, claims, commitments) and a place where upstream's single-batch-opening layout (research-directions §4.2) would pay off. This is inference; the proof was not itemised here.
- Recursion is the other structural lever: with the Akita verifier guest merged upstream, a jolt-atlas proof could in principle be wrapped to a sub-100 KB Jolt proof, at a cost of 10^8-row-scale guest execution for the PCS verifier alone.

### Gaps
- No apples-to-apples lattice-versus-hash comparison on small-integer dense tensors (the zkML workload) exists in the sources read; Table 10 uses field-capacity-dense inputs.
- No current numbers found for lattice folding-based aggregation of many proofs (e.g. per-token proofs) that would quantify the homomorphism advantage for a zkML use.

## 8. Ranked list of what jolt-atlas could adopt, and what remains unknown

### Takeaway
The central question is answered in the negative from the primary source, so the options reduce to proving smallness in the PIOP more cheaply (dense digit columns with a zero-check; an approximate projection check for no-overflow) and to taking upstream's engineering; nothing in the lattice literature read gives exact ranges for free.

### Cited Findings
- Basis for item 1: Akita's degree-b/2 range identity and product tree, with prover cost O(bL). — [Akita paper §2.3, §6.1](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Basis for item 2: random-projection norm checks with constant slack exist with security proofs in the lattice line (Greyhound slack 2, corrected to sqrt(128/29); PikkuFold's concrete modular JL theorem; RoK and Roll's structured projections). — [Akita paper §1.2, §13.2](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf); [ePrint 2026/721](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/721)
- Basis for item 3: upstream one-hot commit and Fp128 kernel PRs merged after the fork's pin. — [a16z/jolt merged PRs](https://github.com/a16z/jolt/pulls?q=is%3Apr+is%3Amerged+merged%3A%3E%3D2026-10-25)
- Basis for item 4: upstream's wider one-hot chunks (4-bit to 8-bit at T ≥ 2^25) increase prover throughput and shrink the proof, at higher verifier time. — [Akita paper §13.4](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)
- Basis for item 5: PR 1911 merged, guest row counts. — [a16z/jolt#1911](https://github.com/a16z/jolt/pull/1911)
- Basis for item 6: Whiteout upstreaming in progress. — [a16z/jolt#2349](https://github.com/a16z/jolt/pull/2349)

### Inferences
Ranked by expected value for the stated bottleneck (25 s of 40 s committing one-hot digits), all subject to the benchmark in item 1:

1. **Benchmark, then possibly adopt, dense small-digit columns with an application-level zero-check in place of one-hot digit polynomials for pure range checks** (remainders, clamp slack). Must enforce: `Q_b(d(x)) = 0` on the hypercube for every digit column and the recomposition identity; needs a narrow dense-bounded Akita preset and catalog. This keeps exact ranges, removes Booleanity/Hamming-weight work for those columns, and shrinks committed domains 16x per 4-bit digit. Not applicable where the digit is also a lookup address (one-hot is needed there). Unknown: dense-path commit time per narrow cell versus one-hot per nonzero.
2. **Approximate no-overflow check for clamp/overflow digits (8 of 12 per einsum output) by random projection at the PIOP layer**, keeping the 4 remainder digits exact. The lattice literature provides the modular-JL soundness template and slack constants; completeness is imperfect unless bounds are set with margin. Independent of the commitment scheme, and already identified in research-directions §7.3; the finding here is that Akita will not supply it and that an exact theorem to cite exists in the lattice line.
3. **Bump the `jolt-akita`/Akita pins to pick up the post-2026-10-24 one-hot commit and Fp128 kernel work.** Lowest risk; directly targets the dominant cost; magnitude unknown (no per-PR numbers were read).
4. **Re-evaluate K=256 one-hot chunks for large tensors**, as upstream does above 2^25 rows: fewer digits per value at higher per-digit table size.
5. **Proof wrapping by the upstream Akita verifier guest** once the atlas verifier is guest-compilable; this is the route to sub-100 KB proofs and on-chain or aggregated verification, the area where the lattice stack beats FlashProve by two orders of magnitude.
6. **Zero knowledge via Whiteout** when it becomes selectable upstream; relevant to private weights or inputs. Not before.
7. **Read LaBinius before the option-C feasibility note**, and SuperNeo/Cyclo/PikkuFold before any folding-across-layers or folding-across-tokens design.

Not recommended on current evidence: relying on the `DenseBounded` flavor for any range or no-overflow property (section 2); adopting LatticeFold+-style monomial range proofs (same cost shape as one-hot); switching to a lattice folding scheme as the main architecture without first reading SuperNeo in full.

### Gaps
- **Unknown, decisive:** relative commit cost on Fp128 of (a) one K=16 one-hot digit, (b) one dense 4-bit cell, (c) one dense 12-bit cell, at GPT-2 trace volume, plus the sum-check cost of the corresponding range checks. No source read gives this.
- **Unknown, primary-source:** the exact norm statement SuperNeo's extractor provides for the folded application witness, its parameters, and its costs (PDF not retrievable this round).
- **Unknown:** exact slack and projection counts for LaBRADOR, RoK and Roll and PikkuFold modular-JL proofs; whether a structured projection with a succinctly evaluable multilinear extension retains the stated slack.
