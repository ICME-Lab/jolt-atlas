# Jolt-atlas overpays for rounding, not arithmetic

No: the way jolt-atlas proves its operations is not the most efficient available, but the inefficiency is concentrated in one place. The arithmetic core is at or ahead of the published state of the art: the einsum engine is Thaler's matmul sumcheck at the minimum round count, intermediate tensors are never committed, and seven structural ops are already zero-round ties. What is expensive is every place a value is rounded, clamped or range-checked, because each one commits one-hot digits and the Akita commitment phase is **25.2 s of the 40.6 s GPT-2 proof (62%)** (`README.md:69-75`). Today a fused einsum/mul output element commits 12 one-hot digits, a biased linear layer 20, an activation 13, a softmax element 22, and an Rsqrt node 33 polynomials with two degree-17 checks; the literature (zkGPT's single range relation for sqrt and division, its constraint fusion worth 1.5x end to end, ZKTorch's one-lookup GELU) and the repo's own clamp-split and bucket machinery show that most of these counts can be cut by 40-60% without changing output bits. The largest levers are: fuse the bias `Add` into the einsum rebase, move every per-node 64-bit range check into the model-wide value buckets, fuse the activation clamp into its table lookup, and test 8-bit one-hot chunks on Akita. One caution frames everything below: **no per-op timing exists on the current Fp128 + Akita backend**, so every saving is a digit-count estimate, not a measurement.

**How to read the evidence.** Three kinds of claim are kept apart throughout. *Code facts* carry a `file:line` reference into the working tree on branch `dev` (2026-11-09, with 34 uncommitted files). Those edits shifted line numbers in the verifier's `clamp_split.rs`, `global_clamp.rs`, `clamp_lookups/mod.rs` and `ops/activation_clamped/mod.rs`; for those files this report cites working-tree lines that were re-read while writing it. Other line references come from the research notes and were spot-checked, not exhaustively re-verified. *Literature claims* carry a URL; several come from abstracts or search summaries only, and are flagged where that matters. *Estimates* are labelled as such and are the authors' arithmetic on chunk counts. Two historic data sources appear and are always labelled: **[MEMO]**, the developer's session memory notes under `/Users/kg/.claude/projects/-Users-kg-swe-icme-official-jolt-atlas/memory/` (Aug-Oct 2026, mostly the retired BN254/Dory backend), and **[TRACE]**, the Chrome trace `trace-1792493663983876.json` at the repo root (Oct 20, Dory, tract importer, a 2,172-node Qwen-like run). Neither transfers quantitatively to Akita. No cargo command was run for this report or for the notes behind it.

## Committed one-hot digits, not sumcheck rounds, set the bill

The only current stage breakdown is the README's GPT-2 run on a MacBook Pro M3: witness + commitment 25.2 s, IOP 5.28 s, opening reduction + Akita openings 6.72 s, total 40.6 s traced and 39.9 s untraced, verify 2.17 s, proof 2.42 MiB (`README.md:69-80`). `docs/upstream-inventory.md:156-158` adds that the commitment phase "dominates (24.4 s), and two of the six 2^24-variable one-hot groups take about 10 s each of it". The retired Dory backend proved the same model in 26.7 s (`README.md:77-78`), so the lattice cut-over moved the bottleneck squarely onto commitment. Akita's own abstract says commitment time is proportional to the number of nonzero entries, not polynomial length ([Akita, ePrint 2026/1983](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1983)). The right cost metric for an op is therefore **committed one-hot nonzeros per element**, then RaVirtual degree (`d + 1` for `d` chunks, `atlas-sumcheck/src/ra_virtual/mod.rs:77-83`), then the number of small sumcheck instances and committed polynomials that feed opening reduction.

This matches an independent analysis of Shout-style zkML: per-lookup proving work "is a function of the access pattern alone, never of the table values", and the one free parameter is the committed address width ([A Separation Principle for Lookup-Based zkML, ePrint 2026/1390](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1390), abstract only). It also matches what the one comparable published breakdown shows on a different stack: in zkGPT, lookups for range and exponentiation are 12.1 s of a 21.8 s GPT-2 proof, all matmuls 1.5 s, and the authors state that "proving range relations is the bottleneck in our system" ([zkGPT, USENIX Security 2025](https://home.cse.ust.hk/~kaichen/papers/zkgpt-security25.pdf)).

All chunk counts follow from four constants: `XLEN = 32`, `LOG_K_CHUNK = 4`, `LOG_K = 64` (`common/src/consts/general.rs:1-7`), default scale 14 (`atlas-trace/src/model.rs:241`), and `instruction_d = ceil(log_K / 4)` (`atlas-sumcheck/src/config.rs:43-46`). Two things stand out. The hot path (fused rescale ops and Add/Sub/Sum) has already been optimised hard: clamp and remainder digits live in packed buckets with no RaVirtual and no read-raf, and a prover-declared per-bucket "exact" flag skips the saturation machinery when nothing saturates (`deferred_lookups.rs:228-292`; `clamp_split.rs:559-627`). Everything outside that path (the `UnsignedLT` range checks, softmax, activation clamps, ReLU, ONNX Clamp) still pays per-node committed chunks, a 32- or 64-address-round read-raf, and a degree-9 or degree-17 RaVirtual, which the clamp-split module itself calls "the most expensive check" (`clamp_split.rs:53-55`).

## Where each op stands against the literature

### Einsum: the contraction is optimal; its rounding is not

Every supported einsum is proven as `acc(r0) = Σ_h L(h)·R(h)·EQ(h)` after folding both operands at the output point, summing only over the contracted index (`ops/einsum/mod.rs:112-131, 180-196`). Rounds are `log k` at degree 2 for `mk,kn->mn` and `k,nk->n`, and `log(ab) + log k` at degree 3 for the batched layouts (`mk_kn_mn.rs:39-45`; `k_nk_n.rs:38-44`; `abmk_abnk_abmn.rs:65-74`): the information-theoretic minimum in each case. This is the same protocol zkLLM and zkGPT use ([zkLLM, arXiv 2404.16109](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.16109v1); [zkGPT](https://home.cse.ust.hk/~kaichen/papers/zkgpt-security25.pdf)), and zkGPT observes that its time "is dominated by computing the bookkeeping table for the input matrices", i.e. the fold. No surveyed system reports a dense-matmul technique that beats it. zkGPT's headline 6.5x on matmul (9.8 s to 1.5 s across GPT-2) is relative to a naive `2·n·m` full-multiplication memoisation that jolt-atlas does not use: the fold here already multiplies field elements by i32 weights with `mul_i64` (`mk_kn_mn.rs:58-75`). As an order-of-magnitude estimate across different hardware and fields, zkGPT's optimised figure is about 15 ns per weight on a 254-bit field against roughly 5 ns here on [MEMO] primitive timings.

What remains is constant-factor work. The dot engine uses none of the repo's own fast machinery: it binds `HighToLow` over dense tables with fully reduced arithmetic, materialises a dense eq table for the batched layout, and allocates a `Vec` per index in the degree-2 kernel (`jolt-atlas-core/src/onnx_proof/ops/einsum/dot.rs`, `round_message` and `bind`; `atlas-algebra/src/poly/multilinear_polynomial.rs:725`), while `SplitEqUnreducedFold` and the delayed-reduction ladder sit unused next door (`atlas-algebra/src/poly/split_eq_poly.rs:51-80`; `atlas-algebra/src/field/mod.rs:180-288`). The fold loops walk weight matrices with stride `k` (`k_nk_n.rs:56-64`) and iterate the padded extent, so a GPT-2 768-by-3072 projection padded to 1024-by-4096 is about 44% zeros. The estimate is 2-4x on fold time from delayed reduction, row-major access and padding skips, and 2-3x on batched-layout rounds from Gruen split-eq; end to end both are bounded by einsum's share of the 5.28 s IOP, so likely 1-2 s on GPT-2 and more on long sequences. The small-value sumcheck of Dao et al. ([ePrint 2026/587](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/587)) does not apply to the rounds, because the folded tables are full-width field elements. A [MEMO] note records that a split-eq `LowToHigh` version of the dot engine existed on an earlier branch and is absent from `dev`.

The real cost of an einsum node is its fused rescale and clamp: 12 committed digits per output element, at roughly 90 ns per hot entry on the M3 by a [MEMO] measurement, against a few nanoseconds per input weight for the fold. And each `Gemm`/`MatMul + bias` lowers to an `Einsum` followed by a separate clamped `Add` (`passes.rs:941-946`), which is 20 digits per output element where 12 would do. Fusing the bias is exact arithmetic: `floor((Σ L·R + b·2^S) / 2^S) = floor(Σ L·R / 2^S) + b` because the added term is a multiple of the divisor, so quotient and remainder are unchanged and the only behavioural difference is `SatClamp(SatClamp(q) + b)` versus `SatClamp(q + b)` when the un-biased quotient saturates. This is the direct analogue of zkGPT's constraint fusion, which cut GPT-2 proving from 33.2 s to 21.8 s and lookup time 1.8x by merging adjacent roundings, with a proof that fusing two roundings never increases quantisation error ([zkGPT, Sections 5.3 and 7.3](https://home.cse.ust.hk/~kaichen/papers/zkgpt-security25.pdf)).

### Rsqrt and LayerNorm: a single range relation replaces two 64-bit lookups

Rsqrt is defined as `isqrt(2^(3·scale) / x)`, zero for `x <= 0` (`atlas-trace/src/semantics/fixed.rs:58-65`), and proven with a degree-3 relation over a committed dense quotient plus two `UnsignedLessThan` lookups on 64-bit interleaved addresses (`ops/rsqrt.rs:61-73, 121-200, 244-275`). zkGPT proves square root with advice and one two-sided range relation, `(2y − 1)^2 <= 4x <= (2y + 1)^2 − 1`, with no sqrt or rsqrt table at all, and does the same for division ([zkGPT](https://home.cse.ust.hk/~kaichen/papers/zkgpt-security25.pdf)). zkLLM instead spends two sequential 2^16 table lookups on the compound inverse-square-root ([zkLLM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.16109)).

The zkGPT pattern transfers with **bit-identical outputs**. Because `floor(sqrt(floor(y))) = floor(sqrt(y))`, today's semantics are equivalent to `out²·x <= S³ < (out+1)²·x`. Two non-negative slacks, each an estimated 38-40 bits at scale 14, replace the quotient and both remainders, and the repo's existing value range check (value sumcheck plus Booleanity linear term, `clamp_split.rs:413-540`) proves them with no read-raf and no RaVirtual. The estimate is 33 polynomials per node becoming about 20-26 per *model* once Rsqrt nodes share a bucket. Three caveats must go into the spec: the relation rises to degree 4; `out` is today bounded only through the lookup operand `2·out + 1`, so the redesign needs an explicit range check on `out` (about 6 chunks) or a downstream bound; and the `x <= 0` case, which the division range check rejects today (`rsqrt.rs:146-152`), must still be excluded. Rsqrt tensors hold one value per normalised row, so the gain is in polynomial and instance count, not committed volume. A tolerance-based (Newton) check is the same relation with a looser bound and makes the output non-unique, which violates the invariant that the verifier never trusts the prover for anything the trace semantics define. A leading-zero-normalised mantissa table needs at least as many chunks and changes output bits. Neither is recommended.

The same value-check treatment applies to MeanOfSquares (`R < N·2^S`, a constant bound: 16 chunks become roughly `2·ceil(k/4)`, about 10 for a 20-bit divisor), the trig teleport remainder (16 to 12) and Div (still 16 chunks, but bucketed and without the read-raf or degree-17 RaVirtual). A [MEMO] census on Qwen counted 2,352 per-node polynomials in the MoS/Sqrt range-check family. The more aggressive zkGPT fusion, merging mean, variance and square root into one per-row relation (their normalisation rounding count halves, 49,224 to 24,608), would also remove the MeanOfSquares remainder but changes the rounding of the intermediate variance and therefore output bits.

### Concat and the other structural ops: three sumchecks that should be ties

Reshape, Slice and Concat each run a full n-round degree-2 sumcheck against a selector table that the verifier materialises at O(2^n) cost. In a compiled model this is unnecessary. Production models are compiled with per-dimension power-of-two padding (`atlas-onnx-import/src/lib.rs:53, 76`), and the compiler already rejects every reshape, slice and concat whose padding would land in the interior (`atlas-trace/src/op/structural.rs:24-85, 191-212, 330-341, 389-419`). Under those checks a Reshape is the identity on the flat padded array, so `out(r) = in(r)` is a zero-round tie. A Slice whose `start` is a multiple of its power-of-two extent only fixes high axis bits, and a Concat whose operands sit at dyadic-aligned offsets is `out(r) = Σ_i eq(r_axis_hi, prefix_i)·in_i(r_rest)`: one claim per operand, no sumcheck, unequal operand sizes handled naturally. A sumcheck is genuinely needed only for a misaligned shift, which the compiler still admits: slices with unaligned `start`, and concats such as `[4]+[8]+[4]`. Even there the selector is a shifted-eq predicate the verifier can evaluate in O(n). This mirrors the GKR view that structured wiring predicates are evaluated by the verifier, not proven ([Thaler, GKR notes](https://people.cs.georgetown.edu/jthaler/gkrnotes.pdf)). The notes found no primary source on how zkLLM, zkGPT or DeepProve treat reshape or concat specifically; ZKTorch uses dedicated Permute and CopyConstraint blocks ([ZKTorch, arXiv 2507.07031](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.07031v2)).

The prover payoff is small. In the [TRACE] run, Reshape, Slice and Concat together cost 0.47 s of a 50.3 s prove over 292 nodes. The case for the change is verifier succinctness, proof size and code deletion. Two neighbours cost more. Per-node evaluation reduction (the GKR line trick generalised to N points, `atlas-sumcheck/src/evaluation_reduction/mod.rs:149-248`) took 1.88 s in the same trace, more than all structural sumchecks combined, because `compute_h` is a sequential loop over heap-allocated per-coefficient polynomials. And the attention graph carries dead NaN plumbing: `IsNaN` lowers to a real node whose relation asserts its output is zero (`ops/is_nan.rs:24-34`), feeding `Where`/`Iff` sumchecks of degree 3 over attention-score-sized tensors (24 IsNan and 48 Iff nodes in [TRACE]). The verifier's `Constant` ties are a separate, known cost: evaluating public weight MLEs was 2.89 s of 9.37 s verify in [TRACE]. Committing weights would make the verifier succinct but adds a large dense opening to every proof, a bad trade while commitment dominates.

### Clamps and range checks: already bucketed, still eight digits wide

The fused-rebase clamp is the most refined protocol in the codebase and nothing in the literature beats it structurally for a one-hot system. Three reductions remain, all estimates.

The first is width. Every output check decomposes `out + 2^31` into eight 4-bit digits whatever the value's true magnitude, and every static bound assumes full-range i32 operands (`atlas-trace/src/op/linear.rs:55-57`; `op/einsum.rs:278-303`). A per-bucket prover-declared width `b < 32` is sound for the same reason the exact flag is (a narrower range is a subset of i32 and implies no saturation), and cuts 8 chunks to 6 at 24 bits or 5 at 20. Alternatively the importer can derive `b` by interval analysis and make it a verifier-known model property. The declared form is complete for whatever the data allows; the static form needs sound bounds for all admissible inputs, and residual-stream nodes will not get useful ones. One range check on the output cannot be removed entirely: with only `R` bounded, a prover can pick a non-integer field element for the quotient, so the output digits are what pin `q` to an integer.

The second is chunk size. `OneHotConfig::validate` already accepts 8-bit chunks (`atlas-sumcheck/src/config.rs:131-140`) and upstream `jolt-akita` ships a K = 256 one-hot family (`third_party/jolt-akita/src/one_hot_family.rs:7-14`), but atlas-pcs hardcodes K = 16 (`atlas-pcs/src/akita.rs:86-89`). With 8-bit chunks a fused element commits 6-7 nonzeros, down from 12. Because the clamp path has no RaVirtual, wider chunks raise no sumcheck degree there, and for the legacy per-node lookups they lower it (17 to 9, 9 to 5). The Dory-era finding that chunk width is a wash [MEMO] was about pairing-based tiers and does not transfer. The unknown is Akita's K = 256 cost per nonzero and whether packaged catalogs cover the needed arities.

The third is the argument itself. LogUp-GKR, used by DeepProve for softmax, GELU and requantisation ([deep-prove README](https://github.com/Lagrange-Labs/deep-prove)), commits only a multiplicity column ([ePrint 2023/1284](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/1284)). It was built and measured here once as a net regression, 46.3 s to 55.4 s, on BN254/Dory [MEMO]. Remainders and quotients are *virtual* in jolt-atlas, so a GKR fractional sum would commit nothing per element; with commitment now 62% of prover time and a 128-bit field, the balance could flip. Separately, the Akita adapter commits dense polynomials under a "dense-bounded" flavour admitting coefficients in `[0, 2^64)` (`atlas-pcs/src/akita.rs:19-25`), and the Akita abstract advertises an "optimized digit range check" ([ePrint 2026/1983](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1983)). If the security theorem guarantees an exact per-coefficient bound for the extracted witness, a narrow dense commitment would be its own range proof and could replace all 12 digits. Lattice extraction usually yields relaxed norms and an ℓ2 bound is not a per-coefficient bound, so this is an open question for the Akita authors, not a plan.

A no-overflow semantic (the trace errors if a quotient leaves i32) would delete the split sumcheck, slack chunks, indicator and exact flag, but buys no prover time, because exact mode already achieves it; a [MEMO] census found zero saturated elements in every Qwen bucket. It trades completeness on saturating inputs for simplicity.

### Softmax: 22 digits per element, 14 of them spent on `z`

The tracer defines softmax by max subtraction, a saturated `z = max − x` split into two 9-bit digits, two sub-table reads multiplied and rescaled with a remainder, a row sum, a verifier-computed reciprocal `floor(S²/sum)`, and a final multiply with a second remainder (`atlas-trace/src/op/softmax.rs:87-149`). `z` is looked up three times: a 32-bit clamp (8 chunks, a 32 + log T round read-raf, a degree-9 RaVirtual), then `z_hi` and `z_lo` (3 + 3). Other systems use the same recipe (ZKTorch and zkGPT both subtract the max and look up the exponential) with two published departures. zkLLM's zkAttn drops the max, argmax and division entirely by letting the prover choose a per-row shift and checking only that each row sums to one within a tolerance, at an L1 error near 10^-2 ([zkLLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.16109)). zkGPT fuses the normalising division into the following product with V, cutting attention roundings from 43,584 to 30,912 in its GPT-2 instance ([zkGPT](https://home.cse.ust.hk/~kaichen/papers/zkgpt-security25.pdf)).

The largest exact win is a single structured lookup on `z`. Because `lut_hi` is zero-padded and the clamp saturates at `z_bound − 1`, every `z >= 2^18` yields `exp_q = 0` (`softmax.rs:60-65, 112-118`); a table over the existing 32-bit address that returns `[z < 2^18]·floor(hi[z >> 9]·lo[z & 511] / S)` reproduces today's outputs bit for bit. That removes the two digit lookups, the exp remainder and its range check, and the ExpProduct relation: an estimated **22 chunks to 12 and 19 instances to about 9**. It needs a prefix-suffix read-raf with a dense 2^18 suffix, whose feasibility in the current `ps_shout` was not checked, and that table exceeds T for small nodes, so it should be one model-global instance. The #218 plan (derive the per-row vectors inside the protocol) is orthogonal: it fixes proof size, verifier work and the zero-knowledge leak of per-row max, argmax and sum, but leaves per-element cost untouched. A public mask selector belongs in the same rewrite, since masked softmax, and therefore any non-power-of-two attention width, is unprovable today (`ops/softmax_last_axis/mod.rs:115-125`; pinned in `op_surface.rs:595-596`).

Priority is lower than the digit counts suggest. Softmax was 0.43 s of 50.3 s in [TRACE] at a 16-token context. It becomes dominant only at long context, where it scales with sequence length squared: an estimated 3-4·10^9 one-hot nonzeros for GPT-2 at 1,024 tokens. For that regime the zkGPT-style fusion into the `·V` einsum stays exact and deterministic, and wins once sequence length exceeds head dimension. The zkLLM shift is the cheapest prover (about 8-9 chunks) but lets the prover choose among adjacent shifts, which requires an explicit relaxation of the repo's core invariant. Base-2, I-BERT polynomial and 2Quad softmax either add remainders or need fine-tuning ([I-BERT, arXiv 2101.01321](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.01321); [Softermax, arXiv 2103.09301](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.09301); [MPCFormer, arXiv 2211.01452](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.01452)) and should not be pursued.

### Activation lookups: the clamp costs more than the table

Tanh, Erf and Sigmoid clamp the input to ±8.0 and index a 2^18 table (`atlas-trace/src/semantics/tables.rs:40-66, 118-127`). Eight of the 13 chunks per element serve a 32-bit clamp lookup whose only job is to reduce the input to 18 bits. The five table-address chunks already prove the clamped value lies in the 18-bit range, so the remaining statement, `clamped = clamp(x)`, is exactly the interior/saturated split the repo uses for saturating clamps, with slack chunks that are empty wherever the input is inside ±8.0. The estimate is **13 dense chunks to 5** per element, two lookups to one, and the removal of a 32 + log T round read-raf and a degree-9 RaVirtual, with output bits unchanged. Packing activation lookups into model-wide buckets then collapses per-node polynomial counts.

Table-structure tricks do not help, which is the separation principle in action. Odd symmetry leaves 17 address bits (still 5 chunks). A piecewise-polynomial form (zkGPT's z-GeLU, NanoZK's interpolated anchors) costs an estimated 7 chunks and a higher-degree relation here; it pays off only in systems whose cost unit is table size or range relations. NanoZK reports a degree-7 global polynomial degrading perplexity by over 100% ([NanoZK, arXiv 2603.18046](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18046), a preprint with internal inconsistencies). What does help is fewer ops around the table: the importer has no GELU fusion pass, so GELU's surrounding Mul/Add nodes each carry their own clamp and remainder digits. ZKTorch fuses the GELU subgraph into a single lookup, "reducing CQs from 6 to 1" ([ZKTorch](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.07031v2)), and zkGPT halves GELU roundings (196,608 to 98,304). A fused `Gelu` table op changes output bits (one rounding instead of several) and needs a linear tail, since GELU does not saturate. ReLU and ONNX Clamp can take the same split treatment, but no model in `models/` contains a `Relu` or `Clip` node (raw ONNX count), so they rank last.

## The ranked list: what to change, in what order

Proposals that several notes made independently are merged here. Ranking is by expected end-to-end effect per unit of risk under the assumption that commitment dominates, which itself rests on one whole-prover breakdown. **Every saving is an estimate from chunk and instance counts; none is measured.** "Bits change" means outputs can differ from today's trace, which requires a `SemVersion` bump and re-pinned goldens in `atlas-onnx-import/tests/golden_zoo.rs`. Every protocol-level row needs the zoo tests and one real-model prove-and-verify run; every new proof field needs a `mutate::Site`; every new relation needs its `Spec` binding and S ⊆ R note.

## What must be measured before any of this is scheduled

The ranking above assumes commitment volume is the cost, on the strength of a single whole-prover table. No per-op, per-family or per-bucket timing exists for Fp128 + Akita anywhere in `docs/`, `specs/` or the README; there is no `benches/` directory; `reports/` was empty before this file. The Dory-era numbers disagree with the current picture in ways that matter: the published Jolt Atlas paper reports commitment at about 3.5 s of about 38 s on HyperKZG ([arXiv 2602.17452](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17452v1)), where it is now about 25 s of about 40 s. Five measurements, most of them one run each, would replace most of the estimates in this report.

The first is a traced release run of GPT-2 and Qwen with `--trace-terminal`. Spans already exist on `Einsum::prove`, `EinsumDotProver::initialize` and the deferred batches, so this settles fold versus rounds, the current cost of evaluation reduction, and softmax's share. The second is an **op-level census of committed one-hot nonzeros**: which fraction of the commit phase belongs to clamp output digits, remainder digits, per-node range checks, activations and softmax, and within the clamp buckets, which producing op (Einsum, bias Add, residual Add, Mul). The notes guess Add/Sub/Sum own 30-50% of clamped elements; rows 1, 4 and 6 cannot be sized without the real number. `ATLAS_AKITA_TRACE=1` reportedly prints a per-class census [MEMO], but classes are keyed by arity, not op, so this needs a small diagnostic. The third is a lowered-graph dump (`dump_lowered_ops`) for each zoo model: node counts per op, the number of biased linear layers, whether any misaligned Slice or Concat survives the importer, and how masks are lowered. Raw ONNX counts overstate structural ops, and the only lowered counts on hand are from the old importer.

The fourth is an Akita microbench at equal information content: K = 16 versus K = 256 one-hot commit and open cost per nonzero, and one dense bounded column versus the equivalent one-hot chunks. It decides row 4 and whether dense-plus-range-argument designs can ever beat one-hot digits. The fifth is a value histogram from the tracer: output magnitudes per node (for declared or static widths), softmax logit spans including mask addends (for a narrower `z`), and saturation counts on GPT-2 and BGE, which are recorded nowhere. Two things should be measured before long-context work is planned: softmax and attention-einsum cost as sequence length grows, and the accuracy effect of the floor-reciprocal `inv_sum`, whose relative truncation error is estimated at up to `N/S` (about 6% at N = 1,024 and scale 14) on diffuse rows.

External comparisons need the same discipline. Headline GPT-2 numbers are not comparable: zkGPT's 21.8 s is for a 32-token sequence on 32 threads, DeepProve's 7.6 minutes is for 512 tokens on 24 cores (and its paper abstract and README disagree, 174 versus 67 tokens per minute), and the Jolt Atlas paper does not state its sequence length ([zkGPT](https://home.cse.ust.hk/~kaichen/papers/zkgpt-security25.pdf); [deep-prove README](https://github.com/Lagrange-Labs/deep-prove); [DeepProve, ePrint 2026/1112](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1112)). Any jolt-atlas claim should state tokens and report seconds per token. DeepProve's paper body and layer docs, and zkAgent's ([ePrint 2026/199](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/199)), were not readable for this survey and are the most valuable unread sources.

## Conclusion

The survey changes the question. jolt-atlas does not need a better matmul or a cleverer lookup argument; on those it is level with or ahead of what is published, and by the separation principle no amount of algebraic structure in a nonlinearity lowers a one-hot lookup's cost. What it needs is fewer rounding points and narrower addresses. Owning the tracer and importer is the advantage competitors lack: semantics can be defined as "the unique integer satisfying this inequality" so that an op is provable by a product and a narrow range check, and adjacent roundings can be fused at import time. The cheapest high-value steps (rows 2, 3, 5, 7 and 8) do this without moving a single output bit.

The lattice backend also turned two settled questions back into open ones. Chunk width and LogUp-GKR were both judged dead ends on Dory; under a PCS whose cost is nonzero count and which may bound coefficients natively, either could be the largest single win or a loss, and only a microbench will say. The sensible order is therefore: take the five measurements, then start at the top of the ranked list with whichever rows the census confirms.
