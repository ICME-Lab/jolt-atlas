# FlashProve's row checks reset the roadmap

The 2026 material points to one change that matters more than all others for jolt-atlas: stop proving per value that a linearly consumed tensor is small, and instead range-check 128 random ternary combinations per row, as FlashProve does, while keeping rounding remainders exact. Read from FlashProve's repository, that check costs 256 looked-up digits per row regardless of row width, so on jolt-atlas shapes it replaces the 8 clamp digits per element with roughly 1,400 digits per row, a 6x cut at width 1,024 and 20-25x at width 4,096, and it works on virtual tensors without changing the commitment scheme (our estimate; nothing is measured). Two hopes in the planning documents are now closed: the Akita paper states outright that its extractor gives no bound of any kind on committed values, and upstream measured 8-bit one-hot digits as slower than 4-bit ones on Akita. One entry is reversed: the "no known construction" hybrid (bits in a binary hash commitment, arithmetic in a prime field) now has published claimants in BitZ and Limber, though both were read only as abstracts. The recommendation is to keep the lattice stack, adopt the row check together with compile-time overflow bounds, take upstream's post-pin work, and let three cheap experiments and three full-paper reads decide whether a bit-committed hybrid replaces one-hot digits later. This will not close the two-to-three-orders-of-magnitude throughput gap to FlashProve; it plausibly takes 10-15 s out of a 40 s GPT-2 proof and preserves what no 2026 competitor has: arbitrary ONNX graphs, exact unique-witness semantics, megabyte-scale proofs and a 128-bit field. The evidence is uneven and is labelled throughout: FlashProve's repository and the Akita paper were read directly, upstream numbers are from pull-request descriptions, and nearly every ePrint paper from July to November 2026 was reachable only as an abstract because the ePrint server refused PDF downloads.

**Evidence labels used below.** *Repo*: read from source or docs of a repository at a named commit, nothing built or run. *Full* or *partial*: body text of a paper read. *Abstract*: abstract page or listing only; numbers are the authors' claims. *Measured upstream*: a number reported in an a16z/jolt pull request. *Our estimate*: arithmetic done in the research notes or here; no jolt-atlas measurement exists for any proposed change. The cost model is the one in the README and roadmap: GPT-2 on an M3 takes 40.6 s, of which 25.2 s is witness and commitment, 5.3 s the IOP and 6.7 s opening reduction and openings; a fused einsum output commits 12 one-hot 4-bit digits (4 remainder, 8 output-range), and Add, Sub and Sum commit 8 each.

## FlashProve proves smallness per row and rounding per value

Everything in this section is read from the repository at commit `9e2bdd8` (release 0.16.0-rc.26, 30 October 2026) unless marked otherwise ([FlashProve](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/tree/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c)). No per-stage prover profile exists anywhere in the repository, guides, paper or technical report, and the written soundness proof of the range test sits in a non-public internal report, so two things the lead engineer would most want (where prover time goes, and the full proof) are not available.

### Field, code and what one committed value costs

FlashProve works over **Goldilocks (p = 2^64 − 2^32 + 1) with every challenge drawn from a cubic extension (X^3 = 2, 192 bits)**, hashes with BLAKE2b, and commits with Reed-Solomon codes and Merkle trees using the zero-knowledge proximity test of Chiesa, Fenzi and Weissenberg (ePrint 2026/391) "with changes" ([gl64.h](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/blocks/field/gl64.h), [protocol.md](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/docs/protocol.md)). The weight table uses a 2^32 domain at rate 0.104 with 2^11 interleaved rows, decoding radius 0.664 (just under the Johnson radius), one out-of-domain sample per row and 62-64 opened columns; each trace segment's first stage is rate 1/2 (2^22 message, 2^23 codeword) tested at δ ≈ 0.29 with 219 positions ([08-parameters.md](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/docs/format/08-parameters.md)). All 341 proximity rounds rest on the beyond-unique-decoding theorem of Ben-Sasson et al. (ePrint 2025/2055); each round reaches 2^-128 only with **20, 28 or 32 bits of proof of work** counted, the weakest round has 96.23 bits without it, and the headline bound is ε(q) ≈ q/2^124 + 2^-101 ([protocol.md](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/docs/protocol.md)).

Trace values are signed bytes, **packed three to a field element at radix 2^20**; wider values are byte limbs, and weights are 13-bit integers held as two signed bytes ([11-1-machinery.md](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/docs/format/11-1-machinery.md)). A committed byte therefore costs a third of a 64-bit message symbol, about two thirds of a codeword symbol at rate 1/2 (our estimate). The extension-field challenge is what lets grand products over 2^30 keys stay near 2^-161: instance size costs FlashProve nothing in soundness, which a 128-bit challenge field cannot say.

### The approximate range check, with its real parameters

The check is the Lyubashevsky-Nguyen-Seiler and Gentry-Halevi-Lyubashevsky random-projection range proof (ePrint 2020/1448 §2.7, 2021/1397 §3.4), applied row-wise. After a segment's packed cells and its "reads" are committed and their out-of-domain samples answered, the verifier draws a matrix Θ with **k columns and τ = 128 combinations, entries 0 with probability 1/2 and ±1 with probability 1/4 each, shared by every row of the matrix**. For each cell row the prover commits the 128 combinations Σ_c Θ[c][u]·cell(i,c), each as a 10-bit and an 8-bit digit of (comb + 2^17), and a dedicated lookup checks all digits against 2^10- and 2^8-row tables. The digits are tied to the committed data by linear equations folded into the segment's single batched claim; the test has no sumcheck of its own ([rng.h](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/blocks/iop/rng.h), [11-1-machinery.md](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/docs/format/11-1-machinery.md)).

What it proves is deliberately loose: every cell lies in **(−2^18, 2^18) when honest cells are signed bytes, a slack of 2^11**, and "the test does not state that a cell is a byte". Its job is to make identities that hold modulo p hold over the integers, together with public no-overflow conditions on the parameters that the verifier tests. The soundness sketch in the source fixes the first "unexplained" column before Θ is drawn and shows each combination passes with probability at most 1/2, giving 2^-128 per round; the ordering is load-bearing ("a prover that knows Theta can alter a read by a vector on which all N combinations vanish"). The authors' own caveats belong in any plan that copies it: the adaptation is "argued on paper and not refereed", a union bound over all tested columns gives 113.44 bits rather than 128, and the round error is machine-checked in Lean while the general window bound "is proved but not applied at the IOP" ([rng.h](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/blocks/iop/rng.h)). Completeness is imperfect: an honest combination leaves its window with probability at most 2e^-128 (below 2^-183.66), an all-zero column of Θ (probability 2^-128 each) voids acceptance, and the bundled completeness error on served requests is 2^-40; a failed request leaks one bit, and the promised retry with fresh randomness is not in the release ([Window.lean](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/lean/Prob/Window.lean), [technical report](https://www.anthropic.com/document/flashprove-technical-report)).

Three properties correct the picture the planning documents drew from the technical report. First, the unit is a row, not a tensor: each row of k cells costs 2τ = 256 digit elements and 256 small lookups whatever k is, so in FlashProve's own terms **the check beats one lookup per cell only when rows are wider than 256 cells**, and its rows run to 8,191 columns. Second, the verifier does not evaluate Θ succinctly; it samples all k·τ trits and does O(k·τ) work per matrix, independent of the number of rows, which is cheap precisely because Θ is shared across rows. Third, the check is not free: on the full-size feed-forward segment the digit array is about 1.28 × 10^8 elements against roughly 4 × 10^8 packed cell elements, a 25-35% commitment overhead that buys the three-to-one packing (our estimate from the layout formulas).

### Rounding stays exact through lookups on virtual keys

The technical report did not say how rounding is kept exact; the repository does. Every rounding site commits the quotient as byte limbs and at most the part of the remainder above bit 16. **The low 16 bits of the remainder are never committed**: exactness comes from one lookup into a 2^16 range table whose key is the virtual linear form 2^lift·(A + 2^(k−1) − 2^k·Q) − 2^16·(high part), where the accumulator A is itself uncommitted and supplied through a Thaler-style inner-product sumcheck. Scaling the key by a power of two makes one table size enforce a range that is not a multiple of the digit width ([11-2-feed-forward-segment.md](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/docs/format/11-2-feed-forward-segment.md)). Quotients that feed a clamp or activation get a second exact check fused with the consumer's table: one 2^16-row table whose rows pack input and output into disjoint bit fields proves both "the input is 16-bit" and "output = f(input)". Variable-denominator division and the norm's integer square root use two-sided checks (R and B − 1 − R), per row for the norm.

The part that matters most for jolt-atlas is what gets no exact check. **Quotients consumed only by linear operations (the residual stream, the attention output, the projection outputs O and D) have no per-value exact range lookup at all**; they are covered by the approximate check and the public no-overflow conditions. That is sound as a statement only because FlashProve's model is a partial function: it is defined where eight kinds of intermediate value stay inside declared intervals, a request outside them "has no proof", and a tool bounds from the weights and scales what any prompt can drive those values to, a bound the docs call "argued, not proved" ([model.md](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/docs/model.md)). There is no saturating clamp on linearly consumed values; clamps exist only as table lookups in front of non-linear consumers.

### Lookups, attention, norm, router and argmax

The lookup argument is a **grand-product multiset equality**, Π(α − key_i) = Π(α − tab_j)^mu_j, reduced along two GKR-style product trees by masked sumchecks. Keys are never sent or committed; only multiplicities are committed, as 32 bit-planes per table row with a "bits are bits" sumcheck, and up to 16 calls with different tables share one lookup through a random shift ([11-1-machinery.md](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/docs/format/11-1-machinery.md)). One research note read the repository's works list as crediting the Papini-Haböck LogUp-GKR paper; the byte-level format shows a product identity with no fractional sums, and that reading is the one used here. Tables have 2^8, 2^10 or 2^16 rows. A feed-forward segment of four layers carries about 2^30 key slots for 1,024 tokens.

Attention is, almost line for line, roadmap item C3 already built. Scores are virtual; the row maximum is the score at a committed argmax index (log T bits, forced not to exceed the query position by its own range lookup); each (query, key) pair commits five byte cells and makes two lookups, one of which is a single fused table that is at once the range check on max − score, the exponential table Tt[u] = rne(2^12·exp(−u/2^8)), the cutoff and the causal mask; normalisation is folded into the ·V division with a two-sided remainder. The address stays near 16 bits because queries and keys are clamped to 12 bits, the difference is shifted by sh = 8, and the table's high rows absorb everything past the cutoff ([11-3-attention-segment.md](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/docs/format/11-3-attention-segment.md)). RMSNorm is proved per token by a row of 99 byte digits carrying the sum of squares, the integer square root, the reciprocal and their two-sided slacks; only the rounding site and clamp are per element. The mixture-of-experts router commits a 0/1 selector per expert, enforces Σ sel = 1, and proves argmax with lowest-index tie-breaking by range-checking mr − lr(e) − Σ_{e' > e} sel(e'). The output head keeps logits virtual and writes, for every vocabulary entry at every position, a margin in three range-checked digits; RoPE is absorbed into the verifier-computed factor of the weight claim and the embedding is a weight claim against a public sparse vector ([11-4-output-head.md](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/docs/format/11-4-output-head.md)).

### Quantization and the measured accuracy price

Scales are per-site, per-layer powers of two fitted on calibration text. Activations are **12-bit after clamping (16-bit before), the residual stream up to 40 bits in five byte limbs, weights 13-bit**; "4-bit weights" names the source file format, not the arithmetic. The integer model's greedy token **differs from the float32 model at 6.81% of positions on 216 texts and 9.41% on the 24 natural passages**, measured on the calibration distribution with no task benchmark, and of 96 greedy replies from two float engines none matches the integer model at every token ([performance.md](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/guide/performance.md), [paper.pdf](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/paper/paper.pdf)). Public tables are generated in float64 and pinned by digest, a weaker discipline than jolt-atlas's integer-only table generation. Measured performance on one H100 with 24 cores is 17.41, 76.34, 111.23 and 123.31 tokens/s at 1, 8, 24 and 64 requests per batch, an 18.78 MB proof, a single-threaded C++ verifier at about 21 s, 39 minutes of one-time weight commitment, and 71-93x overhead against vLLM prefill ([performance.md](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/guide/performance.md)).

### What transfers

The row check transfers, and it transfers to virtual tensors. Nothing in the construction needs the tensor to be a committed codeword; it needs the tensor fixed as a field-valued object before Θ is drawn, and a way to check Σ_c Θ[c][u]·Y[i][c] = Ψ[i][u]. In a sumcheck system the second requirement is one degree-2 sumcheck over the column axis that ends in an ordinary multilinear evaluation claim on Y, which a virtual tensor already supports, plus the verifier's own O(128·k) evaluation of Θ. The first requirement holds for a jolt-atlas output if every hint commitment that determines it (remainder digits, operands' hints) precedes the Θ challenge. This is our analysis, not FlashProve's, and it needs a written proof before use. Sizing for 32-bit honest values gives a window of about 2^40.5 at k = 1,024, eleven 4-bit digits per combination and about 1,400 digits per row against 8k today: **break-even near k ≈ 176, about 6x fewer digits at k = 1,024 and about 22x at k = 4,096**, with rows taken along the longest axis and exact digits kept for narrow tensors such as 16-token attention rows.

The check proves "no wrap-around", not `saturating_clamp_i32`. The proven bound at these widths is near 2^42, harmless in a 128-bit field but above 2^31, so the construction must pair it with one of three things: a compile-time bound showing the clamp can never engage, a declared per-bucket width narrow enough that width times slack stays under 2^31 (honest bound up to about 2^20), or a `SemVersion` change that makes linearly consumed outputs unclamped with the proof failing on overflow. In all three the quotient stays unique, because the exact remainder check and the no-wrap bound together pin it; what changes is behaviour on inputs that would saturate.

The other transfers are confirmations and refinements of the roadmap. Fused tables on scaled keys are B2, B3 and C2 generalised; FlashProve's attention settles two of C3's open questions (address width and table shape); two-sided and scaled-key range checks are B1. What does not transfer directly is the zero-commitment remainder: a Shout one-hot address is the committed object, so a lookup on a virtual key needs a multiplicity-based argument, which over Fp128 costs soundness bits (see the tempering section). Byte-limb packing, the zero-knowledge masking and the link proofs matter only if jolt-atlas adopts a hash-based trace commitment.

## Akita certifies no range, and upstream has moved since the pin

### The theorem answers the open question in the negative

The Akita paper (ePrint 2026/1983; sections 1, 2, 4, 6, 10, 13, 14 and Appendix E read directly) is explicit. Its commitment relation "requires neither the canonical gadget decomposition for s nor membership of the decoded vector in the image of an honest padding map"; the weak opening bounds only the product of the source with a short ring element; and Remark 10.30, "What knowledge extraction does not claim", says the recovered source "contains an inverse challenge difference and need not be the canonical, short gadget decomposition", that a source-shortness claim "does not follow from the bounded folded responses", and that "Akita does not use that stronger claim" ([Akita paper](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)). The extracted root polynomial is therefore an arbitrary F_q vector: **not an exact range, not a slack-relaxed bound, not an ℓ2 bound**. The internal digit range check runs over the next recursive witness (folded responses and inner-image digits), never over the application's coefficients, and one-hot validity is "validated outside the protocol" by the surrounding system. The pinned Akita source says the same from the engineering side: the declared `log_commit_bound` is a producer-side contract whose violation "is not a proof-soundness break" but an unsupported commitment, and the only dense presets on Fp128 are 65-bit and 128-bit (read from the pinned checkout `dfdbd76`, `akita-params/src/sis/committed_source.rs` and `akita-config/src/proof_optimized/fp128.rs`).

What Akita does contribute is a cost property and a template. Dense cost follows declared width, roughly ⌈(b+1)/3⌉ base-8 digits per b-bit coefficient (our estimate from the paper's depth formula), and the paper's own digit check, Σ_x eq(τ,x)·Q(w(x)(w(x)+1)) = 0 at degree b/2 with a product tree above b = 8, is an exact zero-check that an application can run on its own dense digit columns. A range property on committed values must be proved in the PIOP, exactly as one-hot validity is today. SuperNeo (ePrint 2026/242) remains unread beyond its abstract because the PDF could not be retrieved; it is a folding scheme for CCS, not a commitment for a sumcheck IOP, so even a favourable guarantee there would be an architectural change.

The two lineages of approximate range proof now meet. FlashProve's row check and the lattice line (LaBRADOR, Greyhound, Grand Danois, PikkuFold) are the same principle, and the lattice side supplies explicit constants: Greyhound's projection check uses slack 2, which the Akita authors correct to √(128/29) with a sparse-ternary projection, while Akita itself "uses no Johnson-Lindenstrauss projection" ([Akita paper](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)).

On hash-based throughput and size, the usable numbers are these. The Akita paper's own comparison at 2^31 bits committed on eight threads (read directly) has Akita at 0.13-0.35 s commit, 10.5-12.7 ms verification and **63-68 KB proofs**, against WHIR at 0.48 s, 2.1 ms and 113.6 KB, Binius64 BaseFold at 0.8 ms and 489 KB, and Flock Ligerito at 1.1 ms and 495 KB ([Akita paper, Table 10](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)). From abstracts and READMEs: ReedWeave commits 2^24 Goldilocks elements at rate 1/4 in 475 ms on 32 threads with a 595 KiB proof ([ePrint 2026/2147](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2147)), and the Ligerito crate proves 2^24 32-bit elements in 570 ms on 32 threads with a 245 KB proof ([ligerito README](https://docs.rs/crate/ligerito/0.6.2/source/README.md)); that is about 28-35 ns per element on a desktop CPU. FlashProve's weight commitment works out to about 1.4 ns per committed 64-bit message element on an H100 (our estimate from its 2,324 s figure), consistent with an independently measured 134.7 ps encode-and-hash unit cost on the same GPU ([tessera issue #722](https://github.com/AbdelStark/tessera/issues/722)). No measurement exists on an M3, on Apple GPUs for these libraries, or of a hash-based commit against Akita on the same small-integer vector.

The proximity-gap results of 2026 fix the parameters any hash-based component would use, all from abstracts. The up-to-capacity conjectures behind FRI, STIR and WHIR parameter sets were refuted (Crites-Stewart, [ePrint 2025/2046](https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/2046); Krachun-Kazanin-Haböck, [ePrint 2026/782](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/782)); the proven frontier is the Johnson radius with much better error terms ([ePrint 2025/2055](https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/2055)) plus a first sliver beyond it in large characteristic that buys 5-11% of proof size in deployed systems (Dao-Kominers-Thaler, [ePrint 2026/2056](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2056)). The same authors show beyond-Johnson gaps fail on the additive domains binary-field systems use ([ePrint 2026/2424](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2424)), a point for prime fields. The measured price of proven-only parameters is roughly 1.8-1.9x proof size at equal rate (338 against 176 KiB at rate 1/2 in [leanMultisig](https://github.com/scaraven/leanMultisig)). Johnson-regime list sizes also push the challenge field up: FlashProve uses 192 bits, and a 128-bit field would need its own error budget.

For reference, jolt-atlas today is GPT-2 at about 16 tokens on an M3 in 40.6 s with a 2.42 MiB proof and 2.17 s verification (repository README). GPT-2 at 64 and 512 positions on a 32-thread x86 machine is the de facto common workload: DeepProve, zkAgent and zkComposer all report it, and DeepProve takes 34 s at 64 positions there.

Six techniques in these systems are absent from jolt-atlas as documented. They were judged against the planning documents, not against the code.

**Overflow-free scales from weight ℓ1 norms.** zkAgent derives each layer's output scale from B_l = max_j(2^Q·‖W[:,j]‖₁ + |b_j|) and the LayerNorm bound |x̂_k| ≤ √(d−1), so overflow is impossible by construction and no clamp is proven; calibration-based scales, by contrast, overflowed on 0.19-0.54% of GPT-2 tokens, which the paper turns into a 0.66 abort probability for a 512-token transcript ([zkAgent §4.1, App. F](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/199)). FlashProve's tool does the same job from weights and scales. This is the cheapest route to deleting clamp digits with exact outputs, and it pairs naturally with the row check: a public compile-time bound shows the clamp is the identity, and the approximate check supplies the no-wrap bound that makes the quotient the true integer quotient. One prototype's finding is the reason the second half cannot be skipped: "the remainder alone fixes nothing: the divisor is invertible mod P" ([VerInf layergkr notes](https://github.com/JamesPetrie/VerInf)).

**Requantization fused into the next lookup.** DeepProve merges the requant into the following GELU or softmax table, feeding it the unclamped value, and skips requantization entirely when 2q + log M + 1 bits still fit; requant chunks are 27% of its total prover time ([DeepProve B.2, App. C, Table 7](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1112)). This is roadmap C2 with independent evidence of its size.

**One shared model-wide digit table.** DeepProve and zkAgent range-check every shifted chunk against a single 2^10 to 2^12 table with one multiplicity polynomial for the whole model, at 3-5 lookups per requantization. With one-hot addressing this does not transfer as stated, because a wider digit is a wider one-hot polynomial and upstream measured that as slower; it transfers only with a multiplicity-based argument.

**Per-row bracket checks.** VerInf pins the reciprocal square root by y²·S ≥ magic and (y−1)²·S ≤ magic − 1 per row with no table, checks the broadcast product by one random projection per row without committing it, and pins the softmax shift by a two-table monotone bracket with no division ([VerInf paper.md B.3-B.4](https://github.com/JamesPetrie/VerInf)). FlashProve's norm row is the same idea. These keep unique witnesses, unlike DeepProve's tolerance-based row-sum checks.

**Zero-knowledge sampling.** zkAgent is the only 2026 system with implemented top-k, top-p and temperature protocols: a Boolean selector, a hidden separator, masked range checks, exponent lookups and inverse-CDF sampling with a coin derived from the transcript hash, at 0.28 s and 194 KB per generated token against 0.035 s for greedy ([zkAgent §3.3, §5.2](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/199)). Everyone else proves greedy argmax; SASH found co-maximal logits at 15 of 512 positions, so lowest-index tie-breaking is a real requirement.

**LogUp-GKR claims replacing committed cells.** SASH moved five components from committed columns to sumcheck-style claims and removed 38% of committed cells, shrinking its interaction tree from 93,184 to 5,600 columns ([SASH note](https://www.aisafety.sg/research/what-proving-llm-inference-actually-costs-research-note)). This is evidence for jolt-atlas's existing virtual-intermediate choice more than a gap; the stwo-zig "virtual requant remainders" and FlashProve's virtual keys are the part jolt-atlas does not have.

Two further patterns are consensus rather than novelty: one teacher-forced causal pass with shift consistency binding each predicted token to the next input, and boundary-commitment partitioning, where zkComposer measured 4.8-6.8x on 192 cores or 5-8x lower peak memory at 1.4-2.1x time ([zkComposer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08095)). No 2026 system uses recursion or aggregation, none verifies on-chain, none proves sub-quadratic attention, and outside FlashProve none uses an approximate range check. General ONNX coverage is uncontested: the active LLM provers are all bespoke per architecture and the 2023-2025 general frameworks published nothing in 2026.

## Six findings that cool earlier conclusions

**Narrower bit width does not buy proportional proving time.** "Bits Under ZK-LLM" (abstract only) studies nine models and finds activation precision matters far more than weight precision, the RMSNorm inverse-square-root table is a recurring accuracy bottleneck fixed by raising precision there alone, and "lowering bit-width or table size does not reduce end-to-end proving cost proportionally" ([arXiv 2609.36437](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36437)). The systems read in full agree on widths: FlashProve uses 12-bit activations and a 40-bit residual stream and still diverges from float at 6.81% of positions; DeepProve needs 12 bits for GPT-2 parity (perplexity 49.22 float, 60.93 at 8 bits, 49.49 at 12) and Gemma 3 270M collapses at 8 and 10 bits; int8 operands work at 8B-31B only when non-linearities stay in native float (SASH, Attestable). The int8 thesis survives as per-operator precision, not as a uniform width.

**Small-value sumcheck gains under 2x on a two-limb field.** By the papers' own cost model the large-to-small multiplication ratio is about 2N² + 1, roughly 33 for four-limb BN254 and about 9 for Fp128, and an eq factor takes the square root of the gain; the expected speed-up on `eq · small · small` relations is under 2x on a minority of a 5.3 s stage (our estimate from [ePrint 2025/1117](https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/1117), partial read; the extended version 2026/587 was not retrieved). The same paper argues univariate skip is the weaker tool for small integers in a large prime field, and a May 2026 write-up documents a since-fixed verifier omission in Jolt's skip ([zkSecurity](https://blog.zksecurity.xyz/posts/jolt-uniskip-bug/)).

**Eight-bit digits are slower on Akita.** Measured upstream: K = 256 commits in 153.90 s against 80.71 s for K = 16 at 2^29, and the full prover is slower despite a faster sumcheck ([#1994](https://github.com/a16z/jolt/pull/1994)).

**LogUp-style arguments lose soundness bits that sumcheck does not.** Magnetar (read in full) states that a grand-product or grand-sum argument over ℓ elements has error about ℓ/|F|, "so over a 128-bit field even an instance with ℓ = 2^20 loses 20 bits", against about 6 bits for a degree-3 sumcheck, and puts Jolt-Akita at about 122 bits overall ([Magnetar](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/magnetar-1.pdf), ePrint 2026/2423). At 2^24 to 2^28 lookups a LogUp or grand-product component over Fp128 becomes the floor at 100-104 bits. FlashProve escapes by drawing challenges from 192 bits; VerInf, on a bare 64-bit field, reports a LogUp floor near 2^-27.9. Fraction trees are also the most expensive GPU kernel measured (297 ps per step against 135 ps for encode-and-hash) ([tessera issue #722](https://github.com/AbdelStark/tessera/issues/722)).

**Lattice proofs are smaller; lattice verifiers are slower.** On Akita's own table every hash-based baseline except SP1 verifies faster (0.8-3.3 ms against 10.5-12.7 ms at 2^31 bits), while Akita's proofs are 2-15x smaller and its memory several times lower; the authors call it "a proof-size/runtime tradeoff" ([Akita paper §13.3](https://assets.layerzero.network/pdf/akita.pdf)). FlashProve's 21 s verifier and 18.78 MB proof follow from its committed-trace architecture and scale, not from hash-based commitment as such. The size advantage is also being narrowed from the hash side: Canopy reports 20-50% smaller proofs for 5-20% prover overhead ([ePrint 2026/2790](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2790), abstract). Conversely LaBinius's claim of lattice within 1.2x of hash-based on the same binary front end says the dominant factor in Magnetar's 6-20x lead over Jolt-Akita is what gets committed (packed bits against one-hot digits over a 128-bit prime), with hash against lattice second-order on CPU.

**The customer has widened the goal, and private weights have a statement problem.** Anthropic's roadmap update of 19 October 2026 reports a production prototype that "checks that outputs come from a specific set of model weights" and renames the goal from "provable inference" to "inference verification" because the former "suggests a narrower approach"; FlashProve is a separate project ([Anthropic roadmap](https://www.anthropic.com/rsp/roadmap)). The non-SNARK field costs providers 0-2% (fingerprints such as [DiFR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.20621), sampled audits), 1-30% (GPU enclaves) or about 2x (refereed re-execution), and Maverick verifies linear layers with near-zero server overhead ([arXiv 2609.10264](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10264)); all need a verifier that holds the weights or trusts hardware. Per-response cryptographic soundness for a weightless verifier is the property SNARKs hold alone. Hollow-LLM (abstract) shows that with private committed weights a proof of correct inference "is not proof of large-model execution", since ghost weights with collapsing structure pass the same circuit ([arXiv 2607.28884](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28884)); public weights, jolt-atlas's setting, are immune.

Two smaller tempering facts complete the list. FlashProve's "datacentre GPU" limitation eroded within a week of release (a 16 GB consumer-GPU port, an Apple-silicon port, an M31 reimplementation on an M4 Max), so laptop-class hardware is a weak differentiator. And bolting a GPU onto a CPU sumcheck prover yields 1.27-1.64x end to end in the one careful study ([Chimera, ePrint 2026/1388](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1388), abstract), with GPU advantage shrinking as fields widen; a 128-bit prime is a poor fit for the GPU path regardless of commitment.

### The strategic choice

**Option A, adopt a FlashProve-like committed-trace hash architecture for general ONNX.** The case for it is throughput: it is the only design demonstrated within two orders of magnitude of serving cost, the code is open, and a GPU path exists. The case against is specific. It requires leaving Fp128 for a 64-bit or 31-bit field with an extension, since a prime field has no small subfield and a 128-bit field wastes both the hash commitment and the GPU. It gives up megabyte-scale proofs: the hash-based inference provers land at 19 MB to 480 MiB with 20-60 s verification, and SASH at gigabytes. It abandons exact unique-witness semantics unless rebuilt, and it enters a space that FlashProve, three ports, SASH, Sable and an M31 reimplementation occupied within six weeks. Generality would be the only differentiator, pursued from behind.

**Option B, keep the lattice stack and adopt the row check plus upstream's improvements.** This attacks the largest measured cost with no new commitment scheme and keeps every position the survey found unclaimed: arbitrary ONNX, exact semantics, a 128-bit field with no LogUp floor, proofs one to four orders of magnitude smaller than the committed-trace systems (2.4 MiB against 18.78 MB for FlashProve, 135 MiB for Sable and 18.5 GiB for SASH, on different models), and a recursion path that upstream has now merged. Its honest ceiling is a prover perhaps 1.5-3x faster on GPT-2 once ranks 1-8 land (our estimate), not a change of class.

**Option C, a bit-committed hybrid through BitZ or LaBinius.** This is the only route found that could deliver the "commit the accumulator's bits once, derive everything" design with range checks included, and upstream is itself building a binary-field track into Akita. It rests entirely on abstracts.

**Recommendation: B now, with C as the designated successor question and A declined.** The decision between staying on one-hot digits and moving to C turns on three experiments and three reads, all cheap. The experiments: first, a digit census by producing op and row width on GPT-2 and Qwen plus a prototype of the row check on the MLP tensors, which measures how much of the 25 s rank 1 actually removes; second, an Akita microbenchmark of one K=16 one-hot digit against a dense 4-bit cell and a dense 12-bit cell at GPT-2 volume, with the zero-check's sumcheck cost, which decides rank 7 and prices any bit-committed layout on the lattice side; third, a hash-based commit of the same dense small-integer columns on the M3, the like-for-like number no source provides. The reads: BitZ in full, for whether its target ring can be a fixed 128-bit prime and what the cross-ring argument costs per opening; FlashProve's Lean development for the range-test soundness theorem (`lean/Johnson/Desc/First/K8/`) together with PikkuFold's certified ternary Johnson-Lindenstrauss constants, to ground the virtual-tensor proof; and the tensor-train projection paper, in case verifier work on Θ ever needs to be succinct. If the census shows the row check removes most clamp digits and the microbenchmark shows dense narrow cells are not far cheaper than one-hot nonzeros, B is the end state and C stays research. If BitZ's range guarantee is exact over a fixed prime and its commit is an order of magnitude cheaper per bit, C replaces one-hot range digits while the sumcheck layer stays where it is.

## Conclusion

The planning documents framed the choice as lookups and lattices against the field's direction. The 2026 evidence reframes it. The fastest system still does one exact lookup per rounding remainder and one per non-linearity; what it stopped doing is proving, value by value, a bound that only exists to keep field arithmetic honest, and that idea is independent of hashes, GPUs and byte packing. A sumcheck prover with virtual tensors is, if anything, a better host for it than FlashProve's packed commitments, because the tensor is opened by evaluation and the "packing and reads" complication disappears. The lattice commitment turns out to be neither the villain nor the saviour: it certifies nothing about ranges, it is within a small factor of hash-based schemes when fed dense small values, and its real assets are proof size, memory and an upstream that is shipping recursion, zero knowledge and a binary-field track on a weekly cadence.

The decision that remains open is narrower than the one the documents posed. It is no longer "lattice or hash" but "one-hot digits or committed bits" for the values that must stay exact, and it can be settled by a census, two microbenchmarks and one paper read in full. Until those are in, the throughput race at datacentre scale belongs to others, and the position worth defending is the one nobody else in the 2026 survey holds: any ONNX graph, a unique trace, and a proof small enough to wrap and ship.
