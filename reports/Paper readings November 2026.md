# Full texts kill two darlings, fund one experiment

Reading the full papers overturns the shape of option C, the constants of the row check, two sumcheck-engine verdicts and the cost model behind the roadmap's digit-savings table, while leaving the strategic recommendation ("B now") standing on narrower ground than before. Option C is BitZ alone: Limber provides no range check and consumes one, LaBinius's "no bitness check" is a change of meaning (committed values are read modulo 2) rather than a range proof, and neither targets a prime field; BitZ, by contrast, proves an exact per-coefficient width over any finitely generated ring including our fixed 128-bit prime, at a measured **0.52 ns per committed bit** on ten M5 threads (2.12 s single-threaded for 2^30 bits), hash-based and not zero-knowledge, from an AI-optimized proof of concept ([BitZ](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2141), Table 10 p. 64; Remark 2.4 p. 16–17). The row check's soundness is one exact lemma with slack 2 and error 2^-n (LNS Lemma 2.5, p. 8); FlashProve's 2^7→2^18 gap is a completeness-threshold choice our 4-bit digits avoid, the docs' break-even of 176 assumes honest values filling the whole i32 range, and the real figures are 80–112 at 5–7 digits per projection. "Bits Under ZK-LLM" supports an exact alternative we had not priced: per-site power-of-two shifts with per-op clamp widths of 12–16 bits cut 8 clamp digits to 3–4 with no approximation. The separation paper's own ablations show that on the July 2026 HyperKZG backend the per-lookup cost was dominated by ∝m sumcheck folding, not commitment, so every digit-savings number in the roadmap is an upper bound until a per-op profile exists. Univariate skip is Jolt's default and small-value sumcheck scales to 2–3x on Fp128, not under 2x. Sable (open, exact, partial-function, 92.5 s for Qwen2.5-32B at 4K, 135 MiB proofs) and Attestable (closed, ~5 MiB proofs, 250 ms verify, claimed ZK) leave jolt-atlas three unclaimed positions: proof size in Attestable's class with a public soundness story, ONNX generality, and total exact semantics. The prover should now run five measurements in a fixed order (the opening-stage span split and per-op profile, a bit census with width audit and value histograms, the tracer clamp-width sweep, the BitZ-PoC run against an isolated Akita commit, and the D3 digit-base sweep), and the lattice-only end state the team wants must be decided against BitZ's numbers explicitly rather than by default. `agentic_trust.pdf` was deliberately not read.

## Option C shrinks to BitZ, SuperNeo is prior art for D3, and the lattice-only end state is now a decision

**The two docs treat BitZ and Limber as one option; they are opposites.** Limber is a compiler from a field PCS to an integer PCS by limb-splitting: "To commit to f, the prover first limb-splits it into a polynomial f_ℓ whose coefficients are bounded by T, casts f_ℓ into F, and commits to it with PCS. A range check certifies that the committed field polynomial encodes small limbs" ([Limber](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1635), Protocol 5.2 p. 24; Theorem 5.1 p. 23 assumes "any perfectly complete batch range check argument"). Its own Section 8 names range proofs as a major cost (p. 45). Its base field must have characteristic Ω(λ²m²) (p. 2, Remark 5.9 p. 32), so it cannot sit over a binary field (its stated open problem, p. 45), and the note's author transcribed the paper's Appendix A parameter script and ran it at a 128-bit base field: the minimum commitment overhead is **3.1x** at log T = 32 (k = 5), 3.68x at 16 bits, 6.55x at 8 bits; the script reproduces the paper's 256-bit rows (0.762 at k = 6, 0.314 at k = 8) and the 64-bit row (16.0), so the figures follow the paper's own bounds (inference from Table 3 p. 33 and Appendix A p. 51). Limber over Akita would commit at least 4x the witness and still need the range check it was supposed to remove. Its measured numbers (2.1 s for 12,796 constraints single-threaded on an M4 Pro, Table 1 p. 3; 5.4–9.4x over native Spartan, Fig. 3 p. 45) are for cryptographic workloads with non-native arithmetic, which a zkML prover whose PIOP field equals its PCS field does not have. Limber drops out of C.

**LaBinius drops out too, for a different reason.** It commits a binary witness as integer coefficients of Z[x]/(x^162 + x^81 + 1) modulo a composite of 14–15-bit NTT-friendly primes and reads the committed object only through its reduction mod 2, which lands in F_2^162; the key sentence is "The extracted witness over K need not itself have binary coefficients: its reduction modulo 2 defines the binary coefficients to which the arithmetised statement refers. We therefore avoid a separate proof that the committed witness is binary" ([LaBinius](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2103), p. 8). The absence of a bitness check is a consequence of F_2 = {0,1}, not a range proof: nothing bounds the integer coefficients beyond the SIS norm 2β′ needed for binding (Defs. 20–23 p. 17, Thm. 1 p. 22). The paper says "we focus solely on p = 2" and that the extraction strategy "applies to other choices of p" (p. 8), but the reader's inference is that with our 128-bit p the evaluation field becomes R_p = F_{p^162} (a ~20,000-bit field) or a SIMD product with only statistical invertibility, and in either case the committed meaning W mod p is an arbitrary field element, so no range follows. The "within 1.2x" claim is against the Binius codebase's own BaseFold-style prover on Keccak/SHA-256/BLAKE3, single core of an EPYC 9R05 at 100-bit security (ratios 1.12–1.19x; against Flock 0.92–0.93x), with non-recursive proofs of 0.3–5.4 MiB growing as √N and a 0.1–0.7 s verifier after LaBRADOR (Tables 1–6 pp. 28–31). The one number that transfers is a lattice commitment at **0.30 ns per bit single-core** on bit-valued witnesses (inference from Table 1 p. 28: 636 ms for 2^24 F_2^128 elements), which says lattices as such are not the ceiling, what is committed is. There is no author or code overlap with Akita, and whether upstream's #2040 uses LaBinius's decoupling is not determinable from the paper.

**BitZ alone remains, and every condition the docs set for it is met on the paper's evidence.** The commitment object is "a vector bits(f) ∈ {0,1}^ℓ ... packs ... as a vector from F_{2^ν} ... encodes ... with a Reed-Solomon code ... and sends a Merkle root" (BitZ p. 12), instantiated over F_{2^128} with Ligerito at rate 1/2 or 1/8 (p. 62). The target ring is "another arbitrary ring R" that is finitely generated, which includes "All finite fields and the rings Z_n" (p. 1, pp. 28–29). The range guarantee is exact: "a proof for R_LinBitsRings(S, R, ψ) ensures that f belongs to such a subset ... membership to S_{≤B}^{<D} is simply a range check" (Remark 2.4 pp. 16–17; Theorem 4.15 p. 48 proves perfect completeness and round-by-round knowledge soundness, with the relaxation touching only code proximity, not the value range). For our p ≈ 2^128 the skip condition p < (2^128 − 1)/ℓ_1 fails, so Round 1 runs: the prover lifts the claim to Z and the verifier projects to a random prime, with error κ_0 ≤ 2^-105.3 at ℓ = 2^30 and **23 bits of grinding (25.9 ms)** to reach 128 bits, or a larger binary field (Construction 4.4 pp. 36–37; Table 20 p. 82; Remark A.1 p. 82). Round 1's prover cost is O(ℓ) integer additions plus O(ℓ_2) big multiplications (Table 5 p. 39), tens of milliseconds by the reader's estimate; the paper tabulates operations, not times, and gives no end-to-end run with a 128-bit R. Measured on a MacBook Air M5 at 100 bits: 2^30 bits in **561 ms on 10 threads, 2,121 ms on one**, 373 KB proof, 11 ms verifier, 4.0 GB peak; the GKR grand product is 73% of the prover (Table 10 p. 64; p. 13). Not zero-knowledge ("BitZ currently does not support zero-knowledge", p. 65); verifier O(√ℓ) with polylog left open (Remark 2.2 p. 14); the numbers come from a PoC that "was obtained from the production-grade one ... and then asking an AI to optimize the prover" (p. 64), and "Our implementation may not match 100% the optimizations described" (Remark 6.1 p. 50).

Against GPT-2's roughly 2^28 one-hot digits, encoded as 2^30 bits, that is 0.56–2.1 s for commitment plus one opening against about 25 s of witness-and-commitment and 6.7 s of openings on our stack: **15–55x before hardware normalisation, above 5x after a generous 2x discount** (inference). Composition is the standard one: the PIOP over Fp128 ends in ⟨eq(r), ψ(f)⟩ = µ, which is BitZ's native input (§5 pp. 49–50; Remark 4.14 p. 47 for non-tensor weights); every committed small-valued polynomial becomes bit-planes recomposed virtually; the opening reduction collapses many claims to one tensor-structured claim; the Booleanity, Hamming-weight and read-raf machinery on range digits disappears. What the paper does not give is the lookup layer: a Shout one-hot selector over committed bits becomes a product of four bit polynomials per 4-bit chunk, which is roadmap Group E's "four-factor virtual selectors" turned into the design rather than an optimisation (reader's inference from §2.4 p. 22).

**SuperNeo is published prior art for roadmap D3, not for option C.** "Pay-per-bit" in Neo/SuperNeo means that without an NTT (d ≤ 64, |F| ≤ 64 bits) a ring multiplication is a sum of rotations scaled by coefficients, so "the cost to compute the ring operation is essentially adding the rotations rot(a)_i ∈ F^d for which b_i is non-zero" ([Neo](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/242), pp. 15–16). The range guarantee is exact, ‖z^♭‖_∞ < b, and it comes from a product zero-check inside the one sumcheck, NC(X) = Σ_i γ^{i−1} Π_{a=−b+1}^{b−1}(z̃_i(X) − a) (§7.3 pp. 30–31, Lemma 9 p. 43), with recomposition done homomorphically on commitments and claims (§7.5 p. 33); it is mandatory because binding is only relaxed (§2.4 pp. 16–17, Def. 7 p. 20). **The zero-check has degree 2b − 1, which is why every concrete parameter set uses b = 2** (ternary digits; §8 p. 34): a dense 4-bit digit in [0, 16) would need a degree-16 relation, a signed one degree-31. The paper has no implementation, no benchmark, no 128-bit parameter set, and is a folding scheme without an evaluation proof, so Akita's opening would still be needed. This is the mirror image of Akita's situation and exactly "dense tiny digits plus zero-check plus homomorphic recomposition", so the roadmap's D3 gains a design variable (digit base b ∈ {2, 4, 16} against zero-check degree) and loses nothing. Mikros packs a small field into a degree-κ extension and gains the ratio κ = log|E|/log|F| ([Mikros](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2646), p. 2, p. 10); for Fp128 κ = 1, so it does not apply and should not be grouped with the lattice papers.

**The verdicts.** Option A (committed-trace hash architecture for general ONNX) stays declined, and the Sable reading sharpens why: the open, exact committed-trace system lands at 100–500 MiB proofs with 19–56 s single-core verification and one hand-wired gadget composition per architecture ([Sable README](https://github.com/moonmath-ai/Sable/blob/main/README.md)), while the only system in Attestable's proof-size class publishes nothing verifiable. Option B survives, but its mechanism is reframed: the separation paper's ablations (next sections) say the 1.5–3x ceiling, if real, comes from removing sumcheck instances (B1, B2, C2, C3) at least as much as from removing digits, and the fusions C1–C4 survive any commitment choice because they reduce bits as well as digits. Option C is restated as "BitZ-style bit commitment under the existing Fp128 sumcheck layer", moved from "next question" to a funded experiment running in parallel with Group A and B1/B2, with the items that only reduce range-digit counts (D1, D3, parts of B1/B2) flagged as the ones BitZ would make moot. **What the lattice-only end state implies**: adopting BitZ puts a hash-based commitment inside a program whose recorded end state is Fp128 + Akita only and whose recursion path is upstream's Akita verifier guest; verifying Ligerito plus a GKR over F_{2^128} inside an Fp128 guest is non-native, and ZK would have to come from the PIOP side or a future BitZ. That is a strategic conflict, not a technical one, and it should be decided explicitly against the BitZ-PoC number on our M3 rather than by inheritance. The lattice-native way to keep the end state is D3 in SuperNeo's shape, whose per-cell Akita cost for dense small values no paper measures; the D3 microbenchmark therefore gains a third arm (BitZ at 0.52 ns/bit), and if Akita dense cells do not come within about 10x of it, D3 is dominated and the end state has a price tag.

## The row check rests on one exact lemma, and the docs got the constants wrong in the pessimistic direction

**The soundness statement is LNS Lemma 2.5, verbatim**: "Let s ∈ Z_q^m and y ∈ Z_q^n. Then Pr[‖Bs + y‖∞ < ½‖s‖∞ : B ← C^{n×m}] ≤ max{p, 1−p}^n", with C the ternary distribution Pr[0] = p, Pr[±1] = (1−p)/2 ([LNS 2020/1448](https://eprint.iacr.org/2020/1448), §2.7 p. 8; proof as Lemma A.1 pp. 23–24, a two-case conditioning argument that handles wrap-around for |s_i| ≤ q/2). Read with y = 0, threshold T and p = 1/2: **if every projection coordinate is below T then ‖v‖∞ < 2T except with probability 2^-n**, for v fixed before R. The slack is exactly 2 and does not depend on n or k; n sets only the error. GHL restates it as Lemma 3.4 with 128 columns and error 2^-128 ([GHL 2021/1397](https://eprint.iacr.org/2021/1397), §3.4 p. 15) and describes the gap as harmless "because these proofs are only used to show that there is no wraparound modulo P" (p. 15). Two attribution corrections follow. The docs cite "Lyubashevsky–Nguyen–Seiler (ePrint 2020/1448, 2021/1397)"; 2021/1397 is Gentry–Halevi–Lyubashevsky. And the docs say FlashProve's "proof of the test's error is in a non-public report"; the test's soundness is this public, exact, heuristic-free lemma. The only heuristic in the literature was on the completeness side (GHL Corollary 3.2 "[heuristic]", p. 14), which PikkuFold has since made rigorous with certified constants (γ1, α, β, b) = (9.66, 27.37, 343.2, 126) at λ = 128 "at a cost of only a few percent" ([PikkuFold](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1809), Theorem 2 and Table 1 pp. 26–27; §1.2 p. 4). PikkuFold's Remark 4 (p. 27) also settles which test FlashProve runs: an ℓ2 lower tail at 2^-128 is vacuous with 128 rows (the spike vector hits zero with probability 2^-N), so 128 projections identify the ℓ∞ lemma, not a Johnson–Lindenstrauss projection.

**FlashProve's 2^7 → 2^18 gap is mostly completeness margin.** Each honest projection coordinate has variance ‖v‖₂²/2 ≤ k·2^{2w}/2, so a per-coordinate failure of 2^-141 needs T ≈ 13.8·√(k/2)·2^w (GHL's 9.75‖v‖₂ at 2^-141 and PikkuFold's 9.66‖v‖₂ are the same quantity, 13.8σ and 13.7σ), giving an honest-to-proven ℓ∞ slack of 2T/2^w ≈ 19.5√k, which is 2^9.3 at k = 1024 and 2^10.3 at k = 4096 (reader's derivation from GHL Fact 3.1 p. 14 and PikkuFold Thm 2 p. 26). Add the lemma's factor 2 and the 2^11 gap is accounted for. The alternative is deterministic: |y_j| ≤ ‖v‖₁ ≤ k·2^w always, so **T = k·2^w gives perfect completeness at slack 2k**, costing ½log₂k − log₂t + ½ bits more than the probabilistic threshold, 1.7–2.2 bits at k = 1024–4096, which at 4-bit digit granularity is zero or one extra digit. FlashProve's imperfect completeness (≤ 2^-40) and its retry plan are a packing choice (10- and 8-bit digits) rather than a property of the method; in a Fiat–Shamir committed-digits instantiation an honest prover cannot retry anyway (R is a transcript function and the trace is deterministic), so failure means "no proof", which under the ℓ1 rule never happens and under the 13.8σ rule has probability ≤ (coordinates)·2^-141. Nothing is published on failure, so nothing leaks; the masking and rejection sampling of LNS and GHL exist only because their responses are sent in the clear (LNS Fig. 8 p. 35; GHL p. 16).

**The docs' figures (break-even 176, 6x at 1024, 20–25x at 4096) are reproduced exactly by D_y = 11, i.e. by assuming honest values fill the whole i32 range.** At FlashProve-like 2^7 values the figures are 80, 12.8x and 51x; at 2^16, 112, 9.1x and 37x. The "saving" is on the 8 clamp digits only; the 4 exact remainder digits stay, so einsum outputs go from 12 to 4.6–5.4 digits per element, matching the roadmap's "4 + about 1.4 amortised" only at D_y = 11. The value of w is roadmap §2 item 4 (value histograms), or it can be set per op from the compile-time weight-ℓ1 bound of D2, making T a public per-op constant. In GPT-2 the hidden (768) and MLP (3072) rows qualify; attention-score rows have width equal to the sequence length and stay on exact digits until long contexts.

**Sharing one R across rows costs a union bound, and the papers do it.** PikkuFold's Lemma 9 treats block projections I_r ⊗ J with "A union bound over the r_i blocks" (pp. 15–16), and RoK-and-Roll's coarse projection uses the same M in every block ([TTRP](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2146), §5.1 p. 17). So the whole check's error is M_rows·2^-n plus the Schwartz–Zippel terms of the linking sumchecks (~2^-115 on Fp128): **strict 2^-128 over 2^20 rows needs n = 148**; otherwise accept 2^-(128 − log₂M_rows). The cost is linear in n, so this is a 12–16% knob, not a design problem. The field needs only q odd for the lemma; the downstream no-wrap argument needs fan-in × 2T × max weight < q/2, about 2^49 against 2^127 (inference).

**The virtual-tensor protocol and its one precondition.** All four papers project a committed vector and use the commitment for exactly one thing in the proof: fixing the vector before R ("Since M_Z ... is independent of W", TTRP App. A.1 p. 26; "The vector w*_all is fixed before P is sampled", PikkuFold p. 22). In jolt-atlas every virtual tensor is a deterministic field-valued function of public data and the committed digit polynomials, so it is fixed once the witness batch is absorbed. The protocol, as a reader's construction: (1) commit and absorb batch 1 (all one-hot digits); (2) squeeze one R_k ∈ {−1,0,1}^{n×k} per distinct width; (3) compute Y = R·V per row, shift by T and commit its D_y digits as batch 2; (4) range-check the Y digits with the existing machinery; (5) run one eq-weighted degree-2 sumcheck over rows and columns, batched with the op's existing evaluation claim so that a single terminal evaluation Ṽ(r_x, r_z) survives, with the verifier evaluating eq(ρ_x, r_x)·R̃(ρ_u, r_z) itself by O(n·k) signed additions (PikkuFold's batching shape, §3 p. 15 and Fig. 2 p. 18; the block identity MLE[I_r ⊗ M](x, y) = eq(x_0, y_0)·MLE[M](x_1, y_1), p. 24). The soundness argument chains Akita binding (uniqueness of field vectors only; no range property of the commitment is used), the linking identity, LNS 2.5 per row and a union over rows, then GHL Lemma 3.6's step from field identities to integer identities (p. 17). **The precondition is the ordering: R must be drawn after every commitment V depends on.** R is n × k with k ≫ n, so its kernel has dimension ≥ k − n and contains vectors of every magnitude; a prover who could commit part of the witness after R could steer V into ker(R). The current commit-then-prove pipeline satisfies the precondition by construction; any staged-commitment design must re-check it, and the prototype should pin it with a negative test that draws R before the remainder commitment and shows the kernel attack exists.

**Tensor-train projections lose at our widths.** TTRP replaces a dense ternary row by a train of μ cores of rank c, so a row's failure probability rises from 1/2 to 1/2 + (μ−1)/2^{c+1}, the ℓ∞ slack from 2 to 2^μ, the ℓ2 slack to ~2^7–2^10, the proof grows by ~2 KB and the protocol Π⁰ has completeness error 1/2 by Markov (TTRP Lemma 4/Theorem 2 pp. 11–12; Table 1 pp. 4–5; Theorem 4 p. 13). Its regime is whole-witness dimension m = 2^20–2^24 with about 290 rows at p ≈ 2^-90 (Table 4 p. 22). At k = 1024 the reader's extrapolation gives 130 rows instead of 128, slack 4 instead of 2, and ~408k verifier ring operations against ~133k additions for the dense matrix: worse on every axis. It matters only if the verifier becomes a circuit.

**Go, conditional on D2.** The soundness side is one exact lemma and a clean reduction whose single precondition the pipeline already meets; the cost side is 9–13x fewer clamp digits on 768–1024-wide rows and 37–51x on 4096-wide rows at honest magnitudes below 2^16, with perfect completeness at ≤ 1 extra digit; the verifier pays O(128·k) additions per distinct width. What the check cannot do is reproduce saturating-clamp semantics: it is output-preserving only where the clamp never engages, which is D2's job, and if the honest clamp engages anywhere that op keeps exact digits. Two costs the papers cannot price are the second Akita commitment batch inside the opening reduction and the linking sumcheck; both are prototype measurements.

## Per-site shifts with 12–16-bit clamps are the exact alternative, and the tracer settles it without a prover build

"Bits Under ZK-LLM" is a measurement paper: a PyTorch quantize-dequantize simulator for utility across nine models (GPT-2 Small/Medium/XL, TinyLlama, Qwen2.5-3B/7B/14B, Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-30B-A3B) and a modified DeepProve for proving cost on GPT-2 Small/Medium/Large at 16 tokens on 32 threads ([Bits](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36437), pp. 5–6, 15–16). Its quantization is signed symmetric per-tensor with static-max calibration over eight 512-token WikiText-2 windows; power-of-two scales are an ablation (p. 6, p. 15). Its headline utility results: weights at 8 bits cost ≤ 1.2x perplexity on every model, while activations at 8 bits cost 3x to ~500x, and "Most models required at least 12 or 14 bits for the ratio to approach 1" (p. 6). The exact W12A12 vs W16A16 perplexity ratios with exact nonlinearities (Table 9 p. 19): **GPT-2 Small 1.027, Medium 1.024, XL 1.001; TinyLlama 1.144; Qwen2.5-7B 1.165; Llama-3.1-8B 1.725; Qwen2.5-14B 1.080; Qwen3-30B-A3B 1.033**. Dynamic per-token scales recover Llama-3.1-8B to 5.96 (BF16 5.97) and improve 17 of 18 model–dataset pairs (p. 18); power-of-two scales cost 0–5.5% at W12A12 (GPT-2 Small 28.53 vs 27.05) and nothing at W16 ("within approximately 1.2% of the BF16 reference", p. 18). The paper warns that perplexity hides distributional damage: W12A12 has lower KL/Top-5 fidelity than W16A16 on all five large models (Table 8 p. 18; Llama-3.1-8B Top-5 0.653 vs 0.963), and Full12 roughly halves Llama-3.1-8B's MMLU (Fig. 4 p. 18).

**Three claims in the docs are corrected by the full text.** First, "Narrower values do not cut proving cost proportionally" is DeepProve's cost model, not ours: proving time is flat (±5%) from W8 to W16 and A8 to A16 (GPT-2 Small 102.7–109.0 s, Table 6 p. 16) because "different precisions result in the same number of arithmetic operations and lookups" and the "precision-dependent components are too small to materially change total prover time" (p. 9). In jolt-atlas the range decomposition is the dominant stage and costs one committed digit per 4 bits per element, so **bit width is our cost**. Second, "small models collapse at 8–10 bits" inverts the finding: every model collapses at A8, GPT-2 is near baseline from A10–A12, and it is the 7B+ models that need A14–A16 or per-token scales (Fig. 1b p. 6, Table 9 p. 19). Third, "the inverse-square-root table is the recurring bottleneck" is a utility artefact of a fixed-grid LUT: the calibrated RMSNorm input range runs to 65,461 and "66% of the inputs are mapped to the grid point at zero" at B16 (p. 7), destroying 7B+ RMSNorm models (ratios 32–1497, Table 1/5) while GPT-2 is unaffected (1.000–1.002); the fix is a B24 table. jolt-atlas computes rsqrt exactly as ⌊√⌊S³/x⌋⌋ with 2^-14 resolution, 64x finer than their B24 grid (reader's inference against roadmap B1), so the finding describes a reason never to table rsqrt, not a bottleneck of ours. A fourth point the docs lack: the paper's ceiling for cheap integer proving is that products of >16-bit values overflow BabyBear or M31 (p. 9); Fp128 has no such ceiling and carries 32-bit values and i64 accumulators natively.

**What the data supports for us is specific.** Translating "activation bits" to a fixed-step scheme: a tensor with max magnitude a is represented with log₂(a·2^14) + 1 bits at step 2^-14, so our resolution is never worse than their A16 unless a < 2, and the paper's one wide-range tensor (65,461) fits i32 at 2^30 with a 64x finer step than their B24 fix; the prediction (reader's) is that the current scheme matches their W16A16-exact row, within 1.2% of BF16 on nine models. The risk is saturation, not resolution: i32 at 2^14 saturates at |x| ≥ 2^17, and the paper's 24-bit "auxiliary domains" for residual paths (p. 6) plus FlashProve's 40-bit residual stream say the residual stream is the thing to census. Lowering the global scale buys nothing unless the clamp width also drops and costs the +5.5% the PoT column quantifies. Weight precision is irrelevant to our cost (weights are public and uncommitted). The payoff is per-operator: **a per-site power-of-two shift plus a per-op clamp width w lets the output clamp cost ⌈w/4⌉ digits instead of 8; at w = 12–16 that is 3–4 digits, so 7–8 digits per einsum output instead of 12, exact, and independent of row width.** It is a `SemVersion` change (per-site shifts change outputs deterministically) and the exact-semantics alternative to D1/D2; it is also the concrete form of research-directions §8's "rounding placement as a compiler problem" and the honest descendant of the whitepaper's lossy τ.

**Head-to-head with the row check.** The row check reaches 4.6–5.4 digits per einsum output at k = 1024 and better at 4096, but it is approximate (proves ‖v‖∞ < 2T, not the clamp), needs D2 to be output-preserving, adds a second commitment batch and a linking sumcheck, pays nothing below k ≈ 80–112, and leaves attention-score rows (width = sequence length) on exact digits. Per-site clamping reaches 7–8 digits everywhere, is exact, needs no second batch or new relation family, wins on narrow rows and on every Add/Sub/Sum output, and costs a `SemVersion` and an accuracy run. They are complementary rather than exclusive: the per-op width w is exactly the honest bound that sets D_y, so narrowing w to 12–16 bits also drops the row check to 5 digits per projection; and both depend on the same census. The tracer-only experiment that settles the precision question needs no prover build: run `atlas-trace` on GPT-2 (then qwen2-tiny and bge-small) with (a) the current global 2^14 scheme and (b) a per-op saturating clamp of width w ∈ {16, 20, 24, 32} after each fused rescale and Add, with per-op power-of-two shifts from one calibration pass over eight 512-token WikiText-2 windows; record PPL ratio to float, Top-5 overlap on 8,192 scored tokens (the paper's protocol, pp. 6, 17–18), and Σ⌈w/4⌉ + 4 committed digits per einsum output from the value census. The smallest digit count at PPL ratio ≤ 1.01 and Top-5 ≥ 0.96 is the target; if no w < 32 meets it on the residual stream, the answer is "D1 for wide rows, exact 8 digits elsewhere" and the precision question closes. The roadmap's C3 open question ("whether 12-bit clamping holds accuracy on GPT-2-class models") is partly answered (GPT-2 Small 1.027x at W12A12 with static per-tensor scales), but the paper inserts no QDQ after QKᵀ (p. 14), so 12-bit scores specifically remain unmeasured.

## The sumcheck engine's two parked items were parked on a misreading

**Univariate skip over large prime fields is in 2026/587 §7 and is Jolt's default.** The roadmap says "Parked. ePrint 2025/1117 itself argues against it for small integers in prime fields." The extended version states the opposite: "we show that univariate skip can also apply in the setting of small values inside a large prime field, which allows us to apply the technique to applications such as Spartan-in-Jolt" ([2026/587](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/587), §7 p. 18). The construction uses a small symmetric integer domain D_m around 0 with O(m²) interpolation made cheap by small-by-big arithmetic, a packed-width bound BΛ < 2^127 giving K ≤ 14 for 64-bit values (p. 19), and it measures **1.11–1.24x over round batching**, "yielding our fastest linear-space Spartan outer variant (and is the current default in Jolt)" (pp. 22–23). The cost is a higher-degree verifier interpolation (first message degree ≤ 27 for 10 packed constraints, p. 49), which matters for our verifier and for any recursion guest. For us it applies to the first rounds of deferred element-wise batches and Booleanity, not to the folded contraction; the earlier version 2025/1117 was not in `papers/`, so the wording the roadmap relied on is unverified, and §7 supersedes it either way.

**Small-value sumcheck scales to roughly 2–3x on Fp128, not under 2x.** The roadmap's κ (bb-to-ss cost ratio) is confirmed: the paper measures 29x on BN254 (10.50/0.36 ns, Table 3 p. 21) and models κ ≈ 2N² + N (footnote 11 p. 16), i.e. 10 for two limbs against our estimate of 9. But the gain does not scale linearly with κ: Lemma 5/12 gives Θ((d²κ)^{1/δ}) with δ = log₂(d+1) (p. 16, p. 41), so at d = 2 the κ = 10–12 figure is about 0.57 of the κ = 29 figure, and the measured BN254 round-batching gain of 4.10–6.01x (p. 23) scales to roughly 2.3–3.4x; our dearer additions (bb/add is 2.8 on Fp128 against 5.3 on BN254, reader's arithmetic) pull it down to "roughly 2–3x on the affected rounds, unmeasured". The absolute saving stays bounded by those relations' share of the 5.3 s IOP.

**Delayed reduction and the small-by-big fold are the paper's techniques and are roadmap A1/A2 as written.** The roadmap's Section 7 intro says Group E does "not apply to the einsum contraction". Small-value, univariate skip and the high-degree product indeed do not (a(2) = 3 = naive at degree 2, p. 12), but delayed reduction inside a degree-2 round loop measures 1.31–1.41x on BN254 (Table 6 p. 22; only 1.06–1.11x after split-eq, Table 7 p. 23), and the fold Σ_i eq(r,i)·A[i,k] with integer A is the paper's §3.3 primitive including the two-accumulator sign handling (p. 10), measured at 2.2–4.5x over per-term sb and 10–24x over naive (Table 4 p. 21). The paper's Barrett pass is Montgomery-specific; our Solinas reduction differs, so the share of our 4–5 ns multiplication that is reduction is the number to measure. A1's unreduced-accumulation sub-item should expect ~1.1x on the batched layouts, not more.

**The high-degree kernel gives 1.2–1.6x on Fp128 by estimate.** The roadmap's "forward differences and a balanced tree (#1966); 1.7–2.2x" is supported in substance: Procedure 1 splits the factor list in half recursively (p. 12) and Appendix D's extrapolation is a shifted-evaluation recurrence with binomial integer rows such as [24, −1, 4, −6, 4] (pp. 46–47), which is forward differences in all but name, and the paper explicitly drops the earlier Toom-Cook index mappings (p. 15). The 1.7–2.2x is Table 12 (p. 26): 1.74/1.71/1.70/2.18x at degrees 5/9/17/33, BN254, multi-threaded. The exact bb saving is 63 → 31 per point at degree 9 and 255 → 79 at degree 17 (Table 2 p. 14), bought with Θ(d²) additions that cost relatively twice as much on Fp128, so the reader's estimate is 1.2–1.6x; adopt if a microbenchmark of the product of 8 and 16 linears in 16 variables shows above 1.3x, since Groups B and C delete most instances anyway. The paper's implementation is the fork branch `quangvdao/jolt/tree/zkproof-submission` (footnote 5 p. 4), so "bump the pins" is weakened as a source of these kernels until the upstream mainline is checked.

**JaggedPCS is not MosaicPCS.** Research-directions §2 calls JaggedPCS "the published form" of the aligned-subcube layout. It is a different construction: it stacks columns of arbitrary heights into one dense multilinear via cumulative heights sent in the clear and reduces a claim on the stacked polynomial through a product-of-two-multilinears sumcheck costing the prover at most 5·2^m + 2^n + 2^k multiplications, with a verifier that is an O(m·2^k) arithmetic circuit and soundness 2m/|F| ([JaggedPCS 2025/917](https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/917), Theorem 1.4 pp. 6–7); it describes fixed-height power-of-two packing as prior work that "requires substantial overhead in padding" (pp. 9–10), and the PDF carries no venue, so "EUROCRYPT 2026" is unverified from it. With compile-time power-of-two padding, the aligned layout (what `atlas-pcs/src/akita.rs` already does within a class) needs no adapter; Jagged would add **about 0.4–0.6 s on GPT-2's 8 performance cores** by the reader's count (5 multiplications × ~1.5·10^8 cells × 4.5 ns), unless the sumcheck exploited one-hot sparsity, which the paper does not do. Jagged fits over Akita (any multilinear PCS, no homomorphism) and becomes relevant only if padding is dropped or a height-independent verifier circuit is wanted for recursion (p. 7). **The opening-stage saving is bounded by the unmeasured per-class share.** The 6.72 s is "opening reduction sumcheck + Akita class openings" in one span; the reduction sumcheck's prover work is proportional to total opened size and does not shrink by relayout, so only the per-class Akita openings are in play, and the bound is anywhere from under 1 s to 2–3 s depending on a split nobody has measured.

**BinarySpartan contributes nothing new.** It is Spartan over F_{2^128} with Ligerito and Diamond–Posen ring-switching, using only pre-existing optimizations (Phalanx SIMD R1CS, SuperSpartan's `next`, Gruen's skip and eq factorization, Dao–Thaler eq tables, Bagad et al. round computation, Binius64 byte tables; "All but the last are not specific to binary fields") ([BinarySpartan 2026/1656](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1656), p. 3), with 871,796 BLAKE3 compressions/s on an M4 Max (Table 1 p. 7) and no per-constraint cost model, stage breakdown or anything on integer arithmetic. It confirms research-directions §2's Magnetar point ("committing to 128 witness bits is equivalent to committing to a single field element", p. 5) and that a bit-packed binary field is a different product.

## The separation principle is about table values, and its own ablations undercut "digits are the cost"

**The exact statement.** Proposition 5: "In a Shout-style lookup argument the prover's per-lookup cost is a function of the access multiset {aᵢ}ᵢ∈[m] alone, independent of the table values {T[a] = f(a)}. Consequently two activations f ≠ g evaluated on the same quantized inputs incur identical per-lookup cost, and any transformation of f that keeps the lookup acts only on the per-proof term a(r) ... It has zero leverage on the slope b" ([Separation 2026/1390](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1390), p. 20). The kernel is K(f, r) = a(r) + b·m with the per-proof table term κ·c·2^{r/c} (c = 1 dense, c > 1 prefix-suffix) and the per-lookup address term κ_addr·d·m (p. 7, p. 18); Corollary 3 turns this into an Ω(m) commitment floor whose only exits are "fewer operations (a model change) or a commitment scheme that exploits access-pattern compressibility (a different substrate)" (p. 20). The one remaining per-lookup dial is "the address width d = ⌈bits/log₂ k_chunk⌉ ... the committed range of its address space, which must cover the values the computation actually reaches" (p. 21). The roadmap compresses this to "in a one-hot lookup the cost depends on the address width only", which drops the per-proof term and, more importantly, the sumcheck term the paper's own experiments found dominant.

**The ablations.** On the rsqrt node, "collapsing the 1/√ range checks 2 → 1 removes an entire one-hot family (33 → 17 commitments) and 39% of the per-proof constant, yet the slope moves only to 0.90×" (EXP-B3); batching the two range-check sumchecks without removing any gives b ratio 0.993 because "each round still folds every instance's length-m polynomial before combining messages, so the ∝ m work is untouched" (EXP-B4); and "driving the address multiset degenerate (distinct addresses 11,499 → 8, a > 1400× collapse) moves b by only ∼6%" (p. 21). The paper's measurements are all on **jolt-atlas as built in July 2026: HyperKZG over BN254 on Apple Silicon**, before Fp128, Akita and the tracer rewrite (its PR #258 merged 2026-07-07; p. 9; the PR body says "5 fewer HyperKZG commitments"). The July GPT-2 workload proved in 10.4 s there (p. 22), against today's 40.6 s on a different model variant and probably sequence length; the two must be reconciled on one workload before either is quoted. Per-element priors from that backend: a tanh lookup ≈ 14–15 µs, softmax 21 µs per element with a ~50 ms fixed overhead, a 2^64 prefix-suffix range in 82 ms, the dense 2^28 table MLE in 8 s (pp. 11–13). Against ≈ 90 ns per committed nonzero on Akita, 13 digits of a standalone tanh cost ≈ 1.2 µs of commitment, under a tenth of the measured per-element cost in July; either the sumcheck side dominated per lookup then, or the Akita switch inverted the split. **Only a per-op profile distinguishes these, and until it exists the roadmap's digit-savings table is an upper bound on time saved, not an estimate.** The inference strengthens B1 (the read-raf and degree-17 ra-virtualization are rsqrt's cost, not its digits) and weakens any item justified by digit counts alone. The corrected cost model for a lookup node is: κ_commit·d·m + Σ_instances κ_sumcheck(degree)·m + κ_table·c·2^{r/c} + κ_witness·m, with the paper supplying the first and third terms and showing the third is per-proof (m-sweep: 1024x more lookups grew the dense-table cost 1.12x, p. 12), and the split between the first two on Akita being the first thing the census must produce.

**PR #258 provenance.** The "address-width reduction upstreamed" is jolt-atlas PR #258, commit `f5214237e`, merged 2026-07-07: the attention-mask sentinel moved from ≈ −2^30 to −C(ν)·2^ν with C(ν) = ⌈(ν+1) ln 2⌉ + 1, and the softmax SatDiff range-check width from a global 32 bits to log₂(scale) + 8 (the paper measured the +4 form: d 8 → 3, −5 one-hot polynomials per node; merged form d 8 → 4, −4), prover −2.6% to −6.0% on four models, outputs bit-identical (p. 21–22 and footnote 2; PR body). The honest saturation gap on shipped models was 833 (10 bits); post-fused-i64 maxima were 13,773 (GPT-2) and 13,825 (BGE) at scale 8, which answers the roadmap's C3 question about z_acc width with data. The SatDiff family was restructured in the tracer rewrite (#382), so the census must reconcile the roadmap's "22 digits per softmax" with the paper's 16 (July) and 11–12 (post-PR). The general lesson is a width audit per one-hot family against the reachable range, which costs nothing in protocol risk and belongs in Phase 2.

**Theorem 3 rules out sub-quadratic attention.** Research-directions §8 hopes for it because "most pairs contribute exactly zero after the exp cutoff". The paper's Theorem 3/Corollary 2 (pp. 14–17): content-agnostic compression is blocked by a rank gap, and content selection "incurs a prover-work obligation: certifying that the N_seq − k omitted scores per query are not larger touches all keys ... Ω(N²_seq) certified comparisons in total"; "The only escape is approximate, economically-sound selection". Proving a pair is below the cutoff is itself a comparison per pair, so the honest version of our item is "lower the per-pair constant for cut pairs", which is C3. The paper's holonomic (ODE-certified polynomial) erf at 0.27–0.56x the dense lookup (p. 19, p. 21) is a substitution that leaves Shout and is described as "range-check-free" without saying how its output is requantized to a unique integer; the roadmap's "not pursuing" entry should restate its reason as exactness, not "does not reduce digits".

**Celer is a Quark grand product with virtual-key support and O(m·n/|F|) soundness.** Celer proves h(X) = Π(X − aᵢ) equals g(X) = Π(X − Tᵢ)^{eᵢ} at a random point: h(r) by Quark's grand product (committing 2m/log³m packed elements, leaves never committed), g(r) through a Pippenger decomposition of multiplicities into w base-s digits with O(w·(n + s)) committed elements and a small LogUp only at bucket boundaries ([Celer 2026/1453](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1453), §3–4 pp. 8–15; Table 1 p. 5). Prover 10m + O(m^{1/w} + n) field operations (Theorem 1 p. 17; "a sumcheck protocol with degree = 3 on a 2^{log m}-size hypercube consumes 10m field operations", footnote 5). Its input is "Oracle access to ã" and the grand product "reduces to prover's claim on ã(r′), which is verified by V's oracle access to ã" (Protocol 1 p. 16), so **a virtual key works**: a fused-rescale remainder rem = acc − 2^s·q, with acc a sumcheck output and q committed before r, is exactly this shape (inference). The PIOP is PCS-agnostic; Dory is chosen only to open a committed length-m query sublinearly (p. 12), which a virtual key makes unnecessary, so it can sit on Akita, committing nothing of size m. **Soundness error O(m·n/|F|)** (Theorem 2 p. 18), because the cheating prover's digit sums are not constrained to Σe′ᵢ = m, so the degree of g is bounded by n·(m + Θ(m^{1−1/w})) (p. 19): **≈ 84 bits at m = 2^28, n = 2^16 over Fp128**, worse than LogUp's ≈ 100 (degree m + n); a 192-bit extension challenge would restore ≈ 148, or a Σe′ᵢ = m check (reader's suggestion, not in the paper) would recover O((m + n)/|F|). The Llama benchmark (257 s single-threaded on BN254 with Dory for 2^28 lookups against 2^16, ≈ 0.96 µs per lookup; 2.4x over logUp-GKR, 3.8x over Lasso; verifier ≤ 0.11 s, proof ≤ 248 KB, p. 20–21) is a different field, PCS and workload and must not be imported as a speed claim; no ZK construction and no public code are given. For a short-context GPT-2 with m per table at 2^16–2^24, Celer's Pippenger side buys little over committing n multiplicities directly, and the choice is a constant factor on 10m vs 43m field operations. The docs' "the constraint is the challenge field, not the argument family" is partly contradicted: the family sets the constant (log₂(m + n) for LogUp, log₂(m·n) for Celer).

**The single experiment that settles commit-vs-sumcheck dominance per lookup**: on Fp128 + Akita at m = 2^24 virtual 16-bit keys rem = acc − 2^s·q, table [0, 2^16), run (a) today's path (4 one-hot digits through Akita plus Booleanity, ra-virtualization and read-check) against (b) a Quark grand product over r − remᵢ with 2^16 committed multiplicities (logUp-GKR-style side) and optionally (c) Celer's Pippenger side with w = 2, recording prover seconds split into witness/commit/sumcheck, proof bytes and the soundness bound. One run answers the roadmap's open question (does commitment dominate per lookup), D4's viability (it is the only mechanism found that removes the 4 remainder digits per einsum output without changing the commitment scheme), and validates or retires the §1 digit-count savings table.

## Sable is the open, exact committed-trace benchmark; Attestable is a claim

**Sable's design is fully specified** ([Sable README](https://github.com/moonmath-ai/Sable/blob/main/README.md); paper `paper/sable.pdf` at commit `0bc6b54`, release 1.0.0, 2026-11-03, MIT, single author at MoonMath.ai): sumcheck and LogUp-GKR over KoalaBear (p = 2^31 − 2^24 + 1) with challenges in F_{p^4} (≈ 2^124) and commitment challenges in F_{p^8}; rate-1/2 Reed–Solomon, BLAKE3 Merkle trees with a depth-7 cap, mixed-length BaseFold; 103.4-bit round-by-round soundness including a 30-bit proof of work, with one step the author flags as not covered by a stated theorem ("the list-decoding regime, mixed-length batching at later levels and two folds per oracle", §8.2 p. 29). All static lookups go into **one tagged table of 2^26 rows**, so "A range check u ∈ [0, 2^k) is the single lookup (k, u), so every bound costs one lookup whatever its width" (§6.1 p. 17); ShiftRound costs "1 committed element and 2 lookups, plus 1 lookup where the shift can be negative" (§6.2 pp. 17–18); the rounding remainder "never needs its own column" because the LogUp leaf layer is merged with the zerocheck (§7.2 p. 24). Accumulators, attention scores, PV partials, softmax denominators and sums of squares are virtual via product sumchecks; every lookup output is committed, including "one committed probability" per causal score entry (§12 pp. 49–50). LogUp in a 31-bit field is sound only for N < p, so lookups are chunked at 2^30 with per-chunk multiplicities (103–726 chunks) and tuple coordinates get independent challenges (Lemma 8.1). F_O is a partial function (the honest prover aborts out of range; zero aborts on 3.45M real tokens plus adversarial sequences, Table 16), with exact argmax by |V| public-weighted range lookups, exact inverse-CDF sampling from a seeded PRF, and the stopping rule inside the relation (Def. 3.3 p. 10). **Numbers**: Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct 1×4K in **92.5 s, 134.8 MiB proof, 18.8 s verify on one Xeon core**, 16K in 397.5 s and 345.7 MiB, on one RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell 96 GB (Table 4 p. 38), 41–50x its own F_O serving engine and ≈ 90x SGLang FP8; accuracy +8.3% perplexity (5.580 vs 5.151 BF16), 86.3% greedy agreement, MMLU 81.8 vs 83.2 (Table 12 p. 44); queries and keys at 12 bits would remove 37% of the gap at ×1.30–1.68 proving time (Tables 13, 15). Not zero-knowledge ("about 2 x 10^4 proofs together reveal the weights", README); a ZK mode and recursion are designed, not built.

**Attestable publishes claims only.** The blog states "hash functions only", post-quantum, "100 bits of security", zero-knowledge with hidden weights, "Though we prove all non-linear operations in floating points, we quantize all matrix multiplications to integers", a 16K context cap, and a benchmark table on one H100 at 4K context and batch 4: Gemma 4 31B 77.69 tok/s, 5.55 MiB, 251 ms verify; Llama 8B 195 tok/s, 4.94 MiB, 205 ms; Llama 70B 49 tok/s, 5.51 MiB, 239 ms; "These are alpha results" ([Attestable, Proving LLMs at Scale](https://attestable.com/blog/proving-llms-scale)). No proof system, field, code rate, query count, grinding, soundness analysis, wall-clock, memory, paper or code appears anywhere; a third-party analysis found the same absence ([noze.it](https://www.noze.it/en/insights/zero-knowledge-proofs-inference-attestable/)). Reader's inferences, marked as such: the staffing (Papini of LogUp-GKR, Samocha of S-Two) suggests a Circle-STARK lineage over M31; "tok/s" is almost certainly window tokens, so 53 tok/s on a 16K Gemma sequence is ≈ 309 s per proof, within ~1.3x of Sable's 397.5 s on comparable hardware, with a ~25–60x smaller proof and ~100x faster verification, which if true points to proof compression and hiding rather than prover speed as the difference.

**What remains unclaimed for jolt-atlas.** Both systems do things we do not: one causal pass over a whole reply with the stopping rule, committed (and in Attestable's case hidden) weights, 30–70B models at 4K–16K on one GPU, and (Sable) a published per-term soundness budget with a tamper test for every bound. The three positions still open are **proof size in Attestable's class with a public soundness story** (2.4 MiB on GPT-2 against Sable's 106–477 MiB and Attestable's 4.4–7.9 MiB with nothing behind them), **ONNX generality** (Sable is three hand-wired Qwen architectures; Attestable's coverage is unknown), and **total exact semantics** (Sable is exact but partial with an abort path; Attestable's float nonlinearities are unexplained). Sable's cost profile mirrors our thesis ("Commitment, lookups and constraint terms account for almost all of the prover's work", §12) and shows the escape from 4-bit digits that a 2^26-row LogUp-GKR table gives: one committed element plus 2–3 leaves per rounded value, bought with ≥ 124-bit extension challenges and chunking at p, option-space Fp128 has without chunking.

## Conclusion

The abstract-level round had the architecture right and the constants wrong in a consistent direction: it overstated option C's breadth (three papers where one applies), understated the row check (pessimistic by 1.6–2.2x on savings and treating a packing choice as a property of the method), parked two sumcheck techniques the source paper ships as defaults, and read a separation theorem about table values as a cost model about committed digits. The full texts replace "digits are the cost" with a sharper claim that is still unmeasured: on the one backend anyone has profiled per lookup, sumcheck folding dominated commitment, so the 25 s stage has to be split into witness generation, Akita commit and lookup sumchecks before any digit-count saving can be priced in seconds. That split, not any protocol change, is the first thing to run.

Two things are genuinely new. BitZ turns the commitment question from a research direction into a benchmark with a published number to beat, and the lattice-only end state now has to earn its place against 0.52 ns per bit rather than hold it by default; SuperNeo's degree-(2b−1) zero-check gives the lattice side its honest design space (ternary digits, not 4-bit ones). And the Bits paper, read against our cost model rather than DeepProve's, supplies an exact, row-width-independent alternative to the approximate row check that a tracer can evaluate in an afternoon. If the clamp-width sweep lands at 12–16 bits on GPT-2-class models, the row check's remaining territory is the 7B+ residual stream, and the question the team actually faces is whether to commit the remaining 7–8 digits per einsum output as one-hot digits on Akita, as dense ternary digits on Akita, or as bits under a hash tree.
